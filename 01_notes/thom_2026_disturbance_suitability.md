# Thom et al. 2026 — Tree Species Suitability for Regenerating Central European Forests After Disturbance

## Title and Authors
**Coping with novel disturbance regimes: Suitability of 53 tree species for regenerating Central European forests**
Dominik Thom, Marie-Christin Wimmler, Sarah Le Berre et al. — *Forest Ecology and Management*, 2026

## Quick Overview
- **Why is it relevant?** Provides functional trait-based suitability rankings for 53 tree species under novel disturbance regimes — directly relevant to understanding post-disturbance forest composition and species range expansion in the context of climate change.
- **What was done?** Extensive literature review + expert knowledge compiled functional traits (drought tolerance, late frost tolerance, shade tolerance, nutrient/waterlogging requirements) to rank 53 tree species for regeneration suitability on large disturbed areas in Central Europe.
- **What is the main outcome?** Nearly half of 53 assessed species are "suitable" or "highly suitable" for post-disturbance regeneration; many suited species are native to Central Europe; nutrient-poor sites are manageable but waterlogged sites severely restrict options.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept
The 2018–2020 disturbance period (drought + bark beetle) created unprecedented large disturbed areas in Central European forests. Choosing tree species for regeneration under novel conditions (high drought frequency, late frost risk, loss of canopy microclimate buffering) is a major management challenge. This study uses a functional trait-based approach to rank species suitability systematically, including native, southern-range, and exotic species.

## Technical Approach
- **Species:** 53 tree species (native abundant, climate-change-relevant, southern range expansion candidates, North American exotics, Eurasian species)
- **Traits assessed:**
  - Drought tolerance (primary suitability criterion 1)
  - Late frost tolerance (primary suitability criterion 2)
  - Nutrient requirements (site condition filter)
  - Waterlogging tolerance (site condition filter)
  - Shade tolerance (complex forest structure potential)
- **Sources:** Systematic literature review + grey literature + expert knowledge for poorly documented species
- **Output:** Ranked suitability score (highly suitable / suitable / conditionally suitable / less suitable)

## Distinctive Features
- Explicitly addresses the "novel disturbance regime" challenge — conditions beyond historical range of variability
- Includes 8 North American species and several currently southern-European species as climate adaptation options
- Combines regeneration suitability with shade tolerance to assess long-term persistence in structurally complex developing forests
- Transparent ranking table allowing practitioners to filter species by site conditions

## Experimental Setup and Results
- **~50% of species** are "suitable" or "highly suitable" for large disturbed area regeneration
- Top native species include several oaks, beech, larch, and some uncommon natives (e.g., *Sorbus torminalis*, *Acer campestre*)
- Southern European species (*Quercus pubescens*, *Castanea sativa*, *Pinus nigra*) score well on drought tolerance
- Waterlogging tolerance is the most restrictive site filter — only few species tolerate it
- Many suitable species have moderate-high shade tolerance, enabling growth in structurally complex stands

## Advantages and Limitations
- **Advantages:** Practical management tool; comprehensive 53-species scope; transparent trait basis; includes rare/exotic options
- **Limitations:** Qualitative ranking; substantial uncertainty for rare/poorly studied species; trait values derived from literature may not reflect local provenance variation; quantitative experiments needed

## Conclusion
Despite intensifying disturbance regimes, viable tree species regeneration options exist for Central European forests. A diverse portfolio including some southern-range and exotic species enhances climate resilience. The functional trait approach provides practical guidance for foresters, though quantitative species trials are needed to validate rankings under field conditions.
