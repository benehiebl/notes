## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Decoupling of larch growth from temperature in the Central Eastern Alps is modulated by stand density"
- Viktoria Frank, Erich Tasser, Nikolaus Obojes, Walter Oberhuber
- *European Journal of Forest Research* 145: 71 (2026); doi: 10.1007/s10342-026-01912-7

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** Warming was expected to raise growth of temperature-limited subalpine trees, but a "divergence problem" is reported; land-use driven changes in stand density may be one reason.
- **What was done?** Increment cores from 140 European larch (*Larix decidua*) in seven pairs of subalpine stands (one abandoned and densified, one continuously managed and open) in the Central-Eastern Alps were analysed with response functions and a GLMM on basal area increment (BAI).
- **What is the main outcome?** May–August temperature promoted growth in both stand types, but after 2000 BAI no longer followed warming; the decline was stronger with increasing stand density, so density is a key constraint on growth.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: test whether stand density (a consequence of land-use abandonment) modulates the temperature response of larch radial growth.
- Idea: closed stands compete more for water and nutrients, so warming-induced drought stress should appear earlier and stronger there; managed open stands (mowing, grazing, thinning) reduce competition.
- Larch grasslands are a traditional agroforestry system, and larch is light-demanding and periodically hit by the 9-year larch budmoth outbreaks.

## Technical Approach

- **Design**: seven sites (Mieders, Neustift, Navis, Obernberg in North Tyrol; Matsch/Mazia in South Tyrol), each with a paired 1 ha open (managed) and closed (abandoned) stand of similar environment; mean elevation 1723 ± 64 m a.s.l. Pairs were assumed to be historically managed alike because they looked similar on 1970–1974 aerial photographs.
- **Data**: 140 trees cored (ring widths measured with TSAPWin, cross-dated), converted to BAI to reduce age and size effects; stand attributes (basal area, canopy cover, soil depth) and land-use information from landowner communication.
- **Climate**: gridded monthly temperature and precipitation 1920–2022.
- **Analyses**: static and moving response-function analysis (climate–growth stability); a gamma GLMM with log link on individual-tree BAI 1920–2022 (n = 11,651) with climate, site, stand, land-use and budmoth outbreak variables, stand-level random effects, stepwise selection, one-year autoregressive lag; reconstructed basal-area trajectories by linear interpolation between abandonment and 2022.

## Distinctive Features

- Matched-pair design that contrasts managed and abandoned stands under the same site conditions.
- Integrates land-use history and larch-budmoth outbreak years into the growth model.
- Links the divergence problem (usually discussed in boreal sites) to stand density in the Alps.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- BAI of closed stands was higher between about 1985 and 2002; from 2000 the trajectories diverged, BAI declined in closed stands, and open stands approached closed ones from 2004 to 2022.
- Summer (May–August) temperature was the strongest climate driver (maximum correlation 0.30 for temperature vs 0.19 for precipitation); response functions showed no sign of reduced temperature sensitivity in density classes.
- GLMM explained 70.3 % of BAI variance (conditional); previous-year BAI (β = 0.46) and current summer temperature (β = 0.11) had the strongest positive effects; stand basal area had a significant negative effect; summer temperature × July precipitation interaction was positive (β ≈ 0.06), i.e. warm summers help only with sufficient moisture.
- After 2000, warming did not translate into more growth in either stand type, which the authors read as increasing nutrient or water limitation (or optimal temperature reached), intensified by competition in dense stands. Growth in the driest site (Matschertal) was most sensitive to drought.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Controlled pair design and long (1920–2022) records.
- Mixed-effects approach that combines climate, management and disturbance.

**Limitations**
- Land-use history is partly reconstructed from landowner statements, and the assumption of equal prior management in both stands rests on 1970s aerial photographs.
- Correlation of mowing/grazing and thinning was 0.995, so their effects cannot be separated.
- Only living, sampled (surviving) trees are cored, which the authors flag as a sampling bias; only larch, only seven pairs in one region.
- Climate–growth correlations were lower than expected, so unexplained variance is large.
- No direct measurement of water or nutrient status; mechanisms are inferred.
- Subalpine stands (about 1700 m), not the treeline itself, so extension to the treeline ecotone is an assumption.

## Conclusion

- Larch growth in the subalpine zone is limited by summer temperature but no longer tracks warming after 2000, and stand density (a function of past land use) modulates this decoupling.
- Management (thinning, grazing) can buffer the effect.
- Relevance: tree-growth or greenness trends from remote sensing in larch zones should be interpreted with stand density and land-use history in mind. Related: [[obojes_2018_water_stress_growth_larch]], [[obojes_2024_growth_limits_elevational]], [[nicoud_2025_landuse_larch_expansion]].
