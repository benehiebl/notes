## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Drivers of treeline shift in different European mountains"
- Pavel Cudlín and 18 co-authors (SENSFOR COST Action network)
- *Climate Research* 73: 135–150 (2017), Special 34 "SENSFOR: Resilience in sensitive mountain forest ecosystems"

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** Treeline advance is often attributed to warming alone, whereas land-use change is a major confounder; this paper compares drivers across Europe.
- **What was done?** Expert-compiled data on treeline ecotone characteristics, climate change between two periods and land-use history for 15 mountain units (11 mountain areas) from Spain/Italy to Norway/Bulgaria were analysed with redundancy analysis (RDA) and semi-quantitative driver/barrier ratings.
- **What is the main outcome?** Treeline altitude, ecotone width and the importance of climatic and soil barriers decreased with latitude, but most of the remaining variability was attributed to geomorphology, soil, microclimate, land-use history and socio-economic conditions rather than temperature alone.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: evaluate how geographic position, regional climate change and anthropogenic factors affect treeline ecotone features, especially upward treeline shift, and how this interacts with biodiversity.
- Concept: temperature sets treeline position at the global scale, but at landscape scale second-order factors (wind, snow, disturbance, geomorphology, land use) dominate; shifts can be limited by non-climatic barriers (soil, fragmentation, dispersal, ground-vegetation competition).
- The paper treats land abandonment, together with warming, as one of two main drivers of upward treeline movement in Europe.

## Technical Approach

- **Study units**: 15 units in 11 mountain areas, including large massifs (Pyrenees, Alps, Scandes) split into more homogeneous parts (e.g. Central Alps, Eastern Alps) and small isolated ranges (Pirin, Giant Mts.).
- **Data**: expert knowledge and published/personal data by the co-authors summarised in tables (timberline and treeline elevation, species, human influence, observed shift, limits to species shift); climate from the E-OBS gridded dataset (0.25° grid) compared between two periods.
- **Semi-quantitative valuation** of drivers (temperature increase, land-use change) and barriers (drought, geomorphology, natural disturbance, abiotic/biotic/human factors) on a 0–3 scale.
- **Analysis**: redundancy analysis with Monte Carlo permutation tests, explaining (i) treeline ecotone features and (ii) biodiversity loss in forests, meadows and animal communities by geographic position, treeline species, start/decline of human influence and temperature change; supplemented by univariate regressions.

## Distinctive Features

- Pan-European comparison that explicitly sets climate against land-use history.
- Combines treeline shift with biodiversity consequences (forests, meadows, fauna).
- Includes Mediterranean and Balkan mountains, which are rarely compared with Alpine and Scandinavian treelines.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- **RDA 1** (ecotone features): adjusted explained variation 63.2 % (F = 5.8, p = 0.001); latitude, longitude, timberline elevation and the timing of human influence were significant.
- **Treeline shift rates** ranged from 0.43 to 1.9 m yr⁻¹ across the 15 units; shift correlated significantly only with northern latitude, weakly positively with massif size, and not apparently with the climate parameters analysed.
- **Barriers**: climatic and edaphic barriers weaken with latitude; in Mediterranean units late-season drought and winter precipitation deficits (lack of snow) may also limit shifts; rockiness, shallow soils, avalanches and snow gliding were cited as site-based barriers.
- **RDA 2** (biodiversity loss): explanatory variables accounted for 76.3 % of variation; biodiversity loss in forest communities increased with latitude and longitude, and land abandonment was named as potentially more important than temperature for loss of grassland diversity.
- **Interpretation**: forest encroachment in several regions (e.g. Apennines) is linked to pasture abandonment, in line with Gehrig-Fasel et al. 2007; thermophilisation and loss of cold-adapted species were reported.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Broad geographic span and explicit consideration of land use and socio-economics.
- Consistent use of the "treeline ecotone" definition (timberline to tree species limit).

**Limitations**
- Data are heterogeneous, partly expert-based and partly semi-quantitative, with only 15 units, so RDA results are exploratory rather than causal.
- Definitions of treeline/timberline (tree height thresholds, shrubby pine counted as trees) differ by region, which the authors themselves flag.
- Shift rates come from different methods and time spans and are not harmonised.
- The two climate periods are coarse (0.25° grid), which cannot resolve topoclimate near the treeline.
- No remote-sensing based quantification; much of the statement on biodiversity is qualitative.

## Conclusion

- Climate warming alone does not explain treeline shift across Europe; land-use change, geomorphology, soil and microclimate are as important.
- Barriers to shift are strongest in southern and low-latitude mountains.
- For treeline projects: a pure temperature-driven model is not supported by this synthesis, so land-use legacy should be included as a covariate or stratifier. Related: [[dullinger_2004_climate_treeline_shifts]], [[nicoud_2025_landuse_larch_expansion]], [[garbarino_2023_treeline_remote_sensing]].
