## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Modelling climate change-driven treeline shifts: relative effects of temperature increase, dispersal and invasibility"
- Stefan Dullinger, Thomas Dirnböck, Georg Grabherr
- *Journal of Ecology* 92(2): 241–252 (2004)

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** Observed treeline responses to 20th-century warming range from rapid advance to inertia; this paper tests whether dispersal and recruitment limitation can explain the inconsistency.
- **What was done?** A spatially and temporally explicit spread model of dwarf mountain pine (*Pinus mugo*) was parameterised from field data in the Northern Calcareous Alps (Austria) and run for 1000 years under four warming scenarios, two recruitment-kernel shapes and two invasibility patterns.
- **What is the main outcome?** Expansion was slow overall (pine cover rising from 10 % to 24–59 % in 1000 years) and the dispersal-kernel shape and the spatial pattern of resistance by non-woody vegetation influenced range dynamics at least as much as a 0–2 °C difference in warming.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: quantify the relative effects of climatic trend, dispersal shape and invasibility on simulated treeline advance.
- Hypothesis: ambiguous treeline responses partly stem from species-specific dispersal and recruitment limitation and from site-specific resistance of resident vegetation, not only from climate trends.
- The focal species is a shrubby pine, so the result describes a krummholz-type treeline rather than an upright-tree treeline.

## Technical Approach

- **Study landscape**: high mountain landscape of the Northern Calcareous Alps, Austria, where *P. mugo* forms the treeline; mean annual temperature near the summit 0–2 °C, snow cover October–May, a mosaic of woody and non-woody vegetation.
- **Model**: raster-based, model with a long (multi-year) time step of recruitment, growth, fecundity and mortality per site, with clonal layering, so mortality is mainly due to catastrophic disturbance; avalanche paths are unsuitable.
- **Parameterisation**: stratified random field sampling with regression models for growth (231 individuals), fecundity (196 individuals) and 50-year recruitment; abiotic habitat variables from a DEM (radiation, wind, wetness, erosion indices); a site-specific probability of climatic damage.
- **Experiment**: factorial design of 4 temperature scenarios (baseline and increases up to about +2 °C) × 2 recruitment kernels (exponential vs. restricted cubic spline) × invasibility patterns, 10 replicates each (160 runs per outcome), analysed with factorial ANOVA.

## Distinctive Features

- Treats treeline advance as a dispersal- and recruitment-limited spread process, not as an instant climate-envelope shift.
- Empirically parameterised, with explicit demographic parameters measured in the field.
- Systematically quantifies interactions among climate, dispersal and invasibility.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- Pine cover increased from 10 % to 24–59 % after 1000 years depending on scenario; warming effects were strongest when comparing baseline to warming scenarios.
- Dispersal kernel and invasibility together had at least as much effect on range size as the 0–2 °C warming range.
- Strong interaction between kernel shape and invasibility, because grassland swards that are most invasible are spatially clustered just above the current treeline (the belt where warming should favour pine).
- Recruitment close to seed sources was high; a long-tailed kernel increases advance speed.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Mechanistic explanation for lagged and variable treeline responses.
- Empirical demography instead of purely assumed parameters.

**Limitations**
- One species (a shrub), one landscape, and recruitment parameters from the present treeline, so extrapolation above the current treeline is assumed.
- Only temperature is varied; no precipitation, snow, land-use change or disturbance scenarios.
- No external validation of the 1000-year trajectory; the time scale of 1000 years makes the result a statement about sensitivity, not a forecast for this century.
- The shape of the dispersal kernel (especially the tail) is not well constrained by data and was therefore varied as a scenario factor.

## Conclusion

- Slow and spatially variable treeline advance is expected even under warming, because of dispersal and recruitment limitation and site-specific invasibility.
- Remote-sensing-based detection of shifts should therefore not expect a uniform response to climate trends. See also [[cudlin_2017_drivers_treeline_shift]] and [[frei_2023_treeline_shift]].
