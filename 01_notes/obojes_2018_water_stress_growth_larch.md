## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Water stress limits transpiration and growth of European larch up to the lower subalpine belt in an inner-alpine dry valley"
- Nikolaus Obojes, Armin Meurer, Christian Newesely, Erich Tasser, Walter Oberhuber, Stefan Mayr, Ulrike Tappeiner
- *New Phytologist* 220: 460–475 (2018); doi: 10.1111/nph.15348

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** Larch is a widespread Alpine conifer and treeline species; knowing where it is water- rather than temperature-limited indicates where its growth is expected to shift under warming.
- **What was done?** Sap flow and stem radius variations of European larch were measured for three years along an elevation transect (1070–2250 m a.s.l.) in a dry inner-alpine valley in South Tyrol and combined with long-term tree-ring series from five sites.
- **What is the main outcome?** Sap flow and radial growth were reduced in dry periods up to about 1715 m, so maximum annual growth occurred at about 2000 m; long-term growth correlated with precipitation up to 1715 m and with temperature only above about 2000 m.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: quantify how climate and water balance control transpiration and growth of larch along an elevation gradient, and thereby estimate how climate change will affect larch at the dry low-elevation margin.
- Concept (space-for-time): the elevation transect of a dry valley mirrors future conditions of broader regions, with warm and dry low elevations representing the future of cooler, moister higher sites.
- Larch is deciduous and maintains high water consumption through a deep root system, but the study asks whether this protects it from drought.

## Technical Approach

- **Sites**: elevation transect 1070–2250 m a.s.l. in one of the driest valleys of the Alps (sheltered by surrounding mountains); soils shallow (about 30 cm) at most sites.
- **Short-term physiology**: sap flow (thermal-sensor method, six trees per site) and dendrometer stem radius (four trees per site) at S1200, S1700 and S2000 over three years (2012–2014), with soil water content (at 5, 20, 50 cm) and meteorological data; tree water deficit (TWD), daily radius change (DRC).
- **Long-term growth**: tree cores from 10–15 trees at five sites (about 1100 to 2200 m a.s.l.), basal area increment and ring widths against climate with TREECLIM (principal-component-based regression) and moving response functions.
- **Models**: generalised additive mixed models relating sap flow, DRC and TWD to potential evapotranspiration, vapour pressure deficit, precipitation and soil water content (with 1-day lag).

## Distinctive Features

- Links physiological measurements (sap flow, stem radius) with decadal tree-ring data along the same gradient.
- Sites cover the full elevation range of larch in a dry valley, including the lower subalpine belt.
- Includes a contrasting wet year (2014) in which elevational differences vanished.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- In dry periods sap flow and radial growth declined at the lower sites up to 1715 m, where sap flow was limited by soil water content; at the highest site (S2000) no correlation between sap flow and soil water content was found, indicating sufficient water supply.
- Maximum annual growth occurred at about 2000 m; in the wet year 2014 (cool, rainy July) no difference in growth between elevations was observed.
- Daily radius change decreased linearly with vapour pressure deficit at all sites; growth ceased early at the lower sites when water became limiting.
- Tree rings: positive relationship with precipitation (including January snowfall and previous late summer/autumn) at the low sites up to 1715 m, positive relationship to temperature only above 2000 m; at some sites a larch budmoth effect was noted.
- Authors expect an upward shift of the elevational amplitude of larch, with negative effects on silviculture and ecosystem services at low elevation.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Combined physiological and dendro evidence along a continuous gradient.
- High temporal resolution (sap flow, dendrometer) explains the mechanism of limitation.

**Limitations**
- Three years of sap flow and dendrometer data in a single valley, with only three sites in the physiological part; the wet year 2014 was the only strongly contrasting year.
- Space-for-time substitution assumes that elevation effects mirror temporal climate change (it ignores adaptation, differing soils and management at different elevations).
- Tree-ring analysis only on living, dominant trees.
- Only larch, so competition and species replacement are not assessed; the statement about future elevational shifts is an inference.
- Sap-flow upscaling to stand level is uncertain.

## Conclusion

- At dry inner-alpine sites, larch growth is water-limited up to roughly 1700 m and temperature-limited only near 2000 m and above, so best growth conditions have moved to higher elevations.
