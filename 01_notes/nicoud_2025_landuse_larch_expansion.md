## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Climate, not land-use, drives a recent acceleration of larch expansion at the forest-grassland ecotone in the southern French Alps"
- Baptiste Nicoud, Arthur Bayle, Christophe Corona, Rémy Perron Chambard, Loïc Francon, Mathieu Fructus, Marion Bensa, Philippe Choler
- *Science of the Total Environment* 959: 178326 (2025)

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** Treeline advance in European mountains is often attributed either to warming or to land abandonment; few studies have annually resolved tree establishment against both climate and land-use records.
- **What was done?** 628 larch trees along five elevation gradients in the Mercantour region (southern French Alps) were aged by dendrochronology, and breakpoints in age-elevation relationships were compared with bioclimatic indices, demographic records, sheep-stocking history and tracked sheep movements.
- **What is the main outcome?** A wave of establishment starting around 2003 (95 % interval 1995–2010) at about 2300 m a.s.l. coincided with a favourable climate window and not with changes in pastoral pressure; 19th-century abandonment created predisposing conditions.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: determine whether recent larch establishment is driven by climate or by land-use change, and whether grazing intensity affects current tree density.
- Concept: abandonment of 19th–early 20th-century pastoral practices lowered the "climatic treeline", leaving a land-use gap; once climatic conditions open a "window of opportunity", establishment can accelerate in a threshold-like wave.
- Land-use effects were evaluated using demographic, livestock and animal-movement data; climate effects using a composite climate suitability function.

## Technical Approach

- **Study area**: Mercantour area with Annot sandstone, larch forests below about 2300 m a.s.l., grasslands, screes above; extensive sheep grazing; five gradients (Braissette n = 210, Cayolle n = 165, Roche Trouée n = 126, Boucharde n = 50, Eschillon n = 76).
- **Tree data**: 628 oldest-looking trees cored (selected by height, crown size and trunk diameter), covering 2058–2693 m a.s.l.; segmented (breakpoint) quantile regressions of establishment year against elevation (1st, 5th, 10th percentiles and median).
- **Climate**: S2M reanalysis (Météo-France) with a lapse rate of 0.56 °C per 100 m; snow melt-out date, heat and cold waves (cumulative length during the snow-free period), cumulative rainfall; standardised and combined into a climate suitability function; climatic treeline defined by the 6.4 °C growing-season isotherm for 1961–1990 (2375 m) and 1991–2020 (2550 m) with ±0.05 °C uncertainty.
- **Land use**: population censuses (four surrounding towns), pastoral surveys and stocking rates since the 1950s, GPS tracking of sheep (4–8 sheep per pastoral unit) to map grazing/resting vs moving behaviour, and comparison of tree density across stocking quantiles (Wilcoxon tests); historical photographs (1906, 2014) and aerial images (1948, 1983, 2018).

## Distinctive Features

- Annually resolved dating of establishment combined with an explicit land-use data stack (demography, stocking, animal GPS tracking).
- Composite, treeline-relevant bioclimatic indicators (snow melt-out, heat and cold waves) instead of mean temperature only.
- Explicit test of the "land-use gap" idea by comparing the colonisation front with the climatic treeline.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- Trees established progressively at higher elevations; segmented regression (R² = 0.67 for the median relationship) shows a breakpoint of about 2300 m a.s.l. around 2003, uncertainty [1995.2–2010.2].
- The colonisation front in the early 2000s was below the climatic treeline of both 1961–1990 and 1991–2020, i.e. the absence of trees above was related to non-climatic (legacy) factors.
- Population declined sharply 1840–1950 and then stabilised; sheep stocking stayed roughly stable since the 1950s and slightly declined during the establishment acceleration, so pastoral change does not explain the 2000s wave.
- Climate suitability rose from the 1990s with earlier snow melt-out, fewer cold waves and more heat waves; the establishment peak (1995–2005) is synchronous with this window. Tree density showed no clear relationship with sheep stocking in the spatial analysis.
- The authors note that heat during dry summers can reduce survival and that larch seed years (masting) and a cold, wet, late-snow 1990s could have contributed to germination.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Direct, dated evidence of tree establishment at multiple gradients.
- Several independent land-use and climate lines of evidence.

**Limitations**
- Only the oldest, surviving trees are sampled, so earlier establishment events with high mortality are not recorded (survivorship bias); trees shorter than 1 m are included but ages can be underestimated.
- The age at coring height and the establishment date differ, and breakpoint year uncertainty is about ±7 years.
- Correlative attribution: the study "does not fully quantify the relative importance of various climatic variables"; synchrony with a climate index does not prove that climate caused the wave; seed production and dispersal and browsing are not separated.
- Sheep stocking is available only since 1959 and the spatial grazing analysis reflects present-day behaviour; effects of wild ungulates are not assessed.
- One region (extensive sheep pastoral system), so the headline "climate, not land use" is not general; the title also contrasts with the study's own conclusion that land-use legacy set the preconditions.
- Climate series from a reanalysis with a constant lapse rate, which is only an approximation at treeline microsites.

## Conclusion

- Larch establishment accelerated after about 2000 in response to a climate window, but only because land abandonment in the 19th century had previously left the treeline below its climatic potential.
- Interpretations of greening and treeline shifts need to separate legacy effects from recent climate effects; related remote-sensing work from the same group is cited as showing that larch expansion contributes to regional greening ([[bayle_2024_landsat_greening_inflated]] is a caution on Landsat greening trends).
