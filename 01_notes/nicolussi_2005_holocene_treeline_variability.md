## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Holocene tree-line variability in the Kauner Valley, Central Eastern Alps, indicated by dendrochronological analysis of living trees and subfossil logs"
- Kurt Nicolussi, Matthias Kaufmann, Gernot Patzelt, Johannes van der Plicht, Andrea Thurner
- *Vegetation History and Archaeobotany* 14: 221–234 (2005); doi: 10.1007/s00334-005-0013-y

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** It provides an absolutely dated, long-term benchmark for how high the Alpine treeline stood under past climates, which is useful to judge whether present treeline positions are in equilibrium with climate.
- **What was done?** Age and height of living Swiss stone pines (*Pinus cembra*) at one site and tree-ring and radiocarbon analyses of 170 subfossil stone pine logs from five other sites in the innermost Kauner Valley (Tyrol) were combined into a Holocene treeline record.
- **What is the main outcome?** The treeline in the early 19th century was at about 2180 m a.s.l., rose by about 65 m after about 1860 to 2245 m in 1980, and subfossil logs up to 2410 m a.s.l. show that the treeline stood above its 1980 position through most of the period between about 7100 and 2100 b.c.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: build a dendrochronological Holocene treeline record as an alternative to pollen and macrofossil analyses, which are usually dated by radiocarbon alone.
- Concept: tree-ring growth at the timberline is controlled by summer temperature, so the elevation of dated trees is a proxy for past summer temperature; precise (single-year) dating allows comparison with glacier records.

## Technical Approach

- **Study area**: innermost Kauner Valley, Central Eastern Alps, where the timberline ecotone lies at about 2200–2300 m a.s.l.
- **Living trees**: age and height of *P. cembra* in plots at one site (including a tree-species-line and treeline record up to AD 2000); optical height meter for taller trees, ring counts for saplings.
- **Subfossil logs**: 170 stone pine logs from five sites, cross-dated by ring-width series to a continuous chronology back to 5125 b.c. (1-year precision); older logs (about 7100–5040 b.c.) dated by radiocarbon wiggle matching (OxCal 3.9, IntCal98; ±7 years at 95 % probability).
- **Climate**: comparison with regional gridded Alpine temperature data (Böhm et al. 2001) and with the Holocene glacier record.

## Distinctive Features

- Single-year absolute dating of Holocene treeline evidence, rather than ±100-year radiocarbon dates.
- Combines living-tree demography (age, height) with subfossil wood from several elevations.
- Direct comparison with glacier fluctuations.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- **Recent treeline**: low position after the 1810s climatic deterioration; after about 1860 both tree-species-line and treeline rose by roughly 65 m until 1980; the treeline (trees >2 m) stood at 2245 m a.s.l., with saplings up to a tree-species-line of about 2370 m a.s.l. in AD 2000. A group of trees established about 1880 at 2245 m died during the cold 1970s.
- **Holocene**: logs found up to 2410 m a.s.l. date from the last 9000 years; treeline above the 1980 position was nearly continuous between about 7100 and 2100 b.c. (especially 7090–6570, 6040–5850, 5720–5620, 5500–4370, 3510–3350 and 2790–2590 b.c.); individual trees reached ages up to 350 years.
- **Late Holocene**: the dendro record is not continuous over the last 4000 years, probably due to human impact; positions similar to or slightly above 1980 are found for about 1000–640 b.c. and AD 1–330.
- **Outlook**: with summer temperatures as after AD 1980 the authors expect a new treeline at about 2300–2320 m a.s.l., but present temperatures are still below those inferred for long periods of the early and middle Holocene.
- Glacier and treeline records generally agree, but some glacier advances do not coincide with low treeline positions; the authors suggest winter precipitation as a modifier.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Precise chronology and a long record.
- Physiologically grounded proxy for summer temperature.

**Limitations**
- Regional and site-specific (one valley, few sites); a single species (*P. cembra*).
- Preservation bias: wood at certain elevations and sites survives better, and gaps (especially after 2000 b.c.) cannot be separated into human impact and climate.
- The treeline altitude from logs is a minimum estimate of the potential position, and the 1980 equilibrium statement and the 2300–2320 m expectation are inferences, not tested predictions; the lag of the treeline to climate is not quantified.
- Anthropogenic pasture and wood use in the late Holocene confounds interpretation.

## Conclusion

- The treeline in this valley was repeatedly higher than today in the early and middle Holocene, and the 20th century rise brought it back from a very low position after the 1810s, not to an unprecedented one.
- It provides a long-term context for treeline-rise claims and a caveat on land-use confounding. Related: [[dullinger_2004_climate_treeline_shifts]], [[frei_2023_treeline_shift]], [[treeline_ecotone_theory]].
