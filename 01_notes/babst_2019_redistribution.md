# Babst et al. 2019 — Twentieth Century Redistribution in Climatic Drivers of Global Tree Growth

## Title and Authors
**Twentieth century redistribution in climatic drivers of global tree growth**
Flurin Babst, Olivier Bouriaud, Benjamin Poulter, Valerie Trouet, Martin P. Girardin, David C. Frank — *Science Advances* 5: eaat4313 (2019).

## Quick Overview
- **Why is it relevant?** Provides the empirical, global-scale evidence that 20th-century warming has shifted the climatic *limitation* of tree growth from energy to water across large areas — the macro-context behind any regional forest-mortality or productivity work (e.g. [[schuldt_2020_drought_forest]], [[chelli_2017_climate]]).
- **What was done?** Analysed climate-response correlations across 2,710 cross-dated tree-ring sites for two periods (1930–1960 and 1960–1990 CE), spatially interpolated within the climate envelope, and quantified area changes in T-, P-, VPD-, and SPEI-limited zones.
- **What is the main outcome?** Temperature-limited forest area shrunk by −8.7 ± 0.6 Mio km² (−10.8%) between the two periods; VPD-limited area grew by the same amount; the global forest is becoming increasingly water-limited under mild warming.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept
Global vegetation models simulate forest productivity well at sub-annual scales (driven by eddy-covariance + RS) but poorly at annual-to-decadal scales. Babst et al. argue that growth — not just photosynthesis — needs a global empirical baseline. Tree rings provide annual-scale, long-record growth observations across continents. The fundamental question: where are trees energy-limited (respond positively to temperature) vs water-limited (respond positively to precipitation/SPEI, negatively to VPD), and has this map shifted over the 20th century?

## Technical Approach
- Network of 2,710 tree-ring width chronologies covering 55.2% of global land surface (69.9% of present-day forest area).
- Site-level climate response computed against four climate parameters: temperature (T), precipitation (P), vapour pressure deficit (VPD), and standardised precipitation-evapotranspiration index (SPEI).
- Two periods: 1930–1960 and 1960–1990 CE.
- Affinity-propagation clustering of monthly response patterns → 4 main climate-response clusters (cold-humid, hot-dry, cold-dry, temperate).
- Site responses spatially interpolated within the network's climate envelope, then projected into geographic space using gridded growing-season T and annual P.
- Bootstrap uncertainty (1000 random subsets).

## Distinctive Features
- Global-scale empirical synthesis at annual resolution — most analyses of climate response either work site-by-site or aggregate at sub-annual time scales.
- Cluster-based generalisation of monthly response patterns reveals seasonality without prior climate-zone assumptions.
- Direct comparison of two historical periods provides empirical evidence of climate-response shift under realised warming.
- Quantifies area changes in km² and % of represented land.

## Experimental Setup and Results

**Climate-response clusters (1930–1960)**
- Cold-humid: positive T response in summer, weak P response
- Hot-dry: negative T response in summer (heat stress), strong positive P response
- Cold-dry: positive T response in summer, positive P/SPEI response (energy + water co-limited)
- Temperate: positive T response in spring, drought sensitivity in summer

**Spatial extent (1930–1960)**
- T-limited area: 20.8 ± 3.7 Mio km² (25.9 ± 4.6% of network climate envelope)
  - Confined to cold humid regions: southern Alaska, NE Canada, coastal Scandinavia, the Alps, Tibet, NE Siberia
- P-limited area: 77.5 ± 3.3 Mio km² (96.4 ± 4.1%) — nearly universal
- VPD-limited area: 66.0 ± 1.6 Mio km² (82.0 ± 2.0%)
- SPEI-limited area: 78.0 ± 1.3 Mio km² (97.0 ± 1.6%)

**Shifts 1930–1960 → 1960–1990**
- T-limited area: −8.7 ± 0.6 Mio km² (−10.8 ± 0.7%) — substantial decline
- VPD-limited area: +8.7 ± 0.1 Mio km² (+10.8 ± 0.2%) — matching gain
- P-limited area: stable
- SPEI-limited area: stable
- Cold-dry winter T response became *negative* in some areas — likely due to discontinuous snow cover in arid mountain ranges
- Spring T benefited cold-humid and temperate zones (earlier growing season)
- Summer VPD/drought increasingly limited cold-humid, hot-dry, and temperate zones

## Why Annual Precipitation Doesn't Predict Climate Response Spatially
- Growing-season temperature explained 52–79% of variance in interpolated climate response across the network climate envelope
- Annual precipitation explained almost none
- Implication: in the 21st-century warming scenarios, T-driven shifts in growth limitation will dominate spatial patterns, **not** P-driven shifts

## Advantages and Limitations
- **Advantages**: Unprecedented global coverage of growth observations; explicit climate-envelope interpolation with bootstrap uncertainty; clear quantification of area shifts.
- **Limitations**: Tree-ring network biased toward old-growth and climate-sensitive sites (under-represents tropics, sub-tropics); period comparison stops in 1990 — does not include the rapid 21st-century warming; some warm-humid climates (Amazon, Indonesia, central Africa) sparsely represented; no biotic interactions (competition, disturbance, mortality) included.

## Conclusion
**20th-century warming triggered a measurable global redistribution in the climatic drivers of tree growth.** The energy-limited fraction of forest area shrank by ≈11% (mostly converting to VPD-limited). With continued warming, this redistribution will accelerate and may trigger biome shifts. The result establishes the empirical foundation for water-limited regimes becoming the dominant constraint on global tree productivity — a foundational reference for any climate-impact analysis of forest growth or distribution.
