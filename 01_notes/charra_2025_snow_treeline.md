## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Reduced snow cover at the alpine treeline: resistance and recovery of saplings"
- Katline Charra-Vaskou, Guillaume Charrier, Andrea Ganthaler, Thierry Améglio, Stefan Mayr
- Published in *New Phytologist* (2026) 250: 1492–1509; doi: 10.1111/nph.70926
- Received 30 April 2025, accepted 26 December 2025 (note: source filename tags this as a 2025 paper, but the journal volume/print year is 2026)

## Quick Overview
- **Why is it relevant?** It directly tests how reduced snow cover — an expected consequence of climate change — affects survival, growth and physiology of tree/shrub saplings at the alpine treeline, a core topic for the wiki's treeline ecotone focus.
- **What was done?** A two-winter snow-removal experiment at 1700 m asl (Tyrolean Central Alps) compared saplings of 5 woody species (2 angiosperm trees, 1 conifer shrub, 2 conifer trees) kept snow-covered vs made snow-free, monitoring stem diameter, hydraulic conductivity loss, cell mortality, carbohydrates, growth and survival.
- **What is the main outcome?** Snow-free saplings suffered significantly more hydraulic damage, cell mortality and summer mortality, and less growth, than snow-covered saplings, but species differed strongly in their resistance and recovery strategies (resprouting, embolism refilling, positive stem pressure).

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Winter snow cover normally insulates saplings/shrubs from frost, freeze-thaw cycles (FTEs) and desiccation; climate change is expected to shorten and thin snow cover, paradoxically increasing frost/drought exposure despite warmer mean temperatures.
- The study aimed to determine which ecophysiological parameters (hydraulic integrity, living-cell mortality, carbohydrate mobilization) explain sapling survival and growth loss under snow-free (SF) winter conditions, and to distinguish species' **resistance** (ability to withstand stress) from **recovery** (ability to restore function afterward) at the treeline.
- Three hypotheses tested: (1) lack of snow cover induces frost damage to living cells and hydraulic compartments; (2) different species react according to species-specific physiological thresholds; (3) survival to SF winters depends on both hydraulic/cell damage and the ability to mobilize carbohydrates for regrowth.

## Technical Approach

- Field snow-removal experiment at Praxmar, Sellrain, Tyrolean Central Alps, Austria (1700 m asl), Nov 2021–Oct 2022.
- 100 saplings (2–4 yr old, 50 cm–1.5 m) of 5 species: *Acer pseudoplatanus*, *Sorbus aucuparia* (angiosperm trees), *Juniperus communis* (conifer shrub), *Larix decidua*, *Picea abies* (conifer trees) — chosen to contrast angiosperm/conifer, deciduous/evergreen, and tall/shrub growth forms.
- Two plots: snow-free (SF, hand-removed daily) vs naturally snow-covered (SC), Nov 1–March 25.
- Continuous measurements: stem diameter (LVDT sensors), stem/soil/air temperature, soil water potential, snow depth.
- Spring/summer destructive and physiological measurements: percent loss of hydraulic conductivity (PLC, via high-resolution X-ray computed tomography), percent loss of diameter (PLD, an index of living-cell mortality), relative electrolyte leakage (REL), nonstructural carbohydrates (starch, sucrose, glucose, fructose), and a visually-scored summer mortality index (MI, 1=healthy to 5=dead, including a "resprouting" category).
- Statistics: Kruskal-Wallis tests, Pearson correlations, and generalized linear models comparing physiological vs qualitative (species/growth-form) predictors of mortality and growth, evaluated via RMSE, adjusted R², AICc and leave-one-out cross-validation.

## Distinctive Features

- Combines continuous nondestructive dendrometry with destructive physiological sampling (HRCT-based embolism imaging, carbohydrate assays) across a genuinely broad functional spectrum (5 species spanning angiosperm/conifer, evergreen/deciduous, tree/shrub).
- Explicitly separates **resistance** (surviving the stress) from **recovery** (restoring function afterward) as distinct, quantifiable indices — an alternative framing to the coarser "resilience" concept used in prior large-scale studies.
- Identifies species-specific coping strategies: resprouting from the root system (*Sorbus*, *Acer*), embolism refilling (*Picea*), and positive stem pressure limiting cavitation (*Acer*).
- Best predictive model of summer mortality combined physiological variables (spring hydraulic loss, spring diameter loss, spring sucrose and starch content) and outperformed a model based only on experimental/species factors (adjusted efficiency 0.895 vs 0.604), suggesting mortality risk is mechanistically tied to combined hydraulic failure, cell mortality and carbon allocation rather than being simply species-predictable.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- Snow-free plots experienced 53 freeze-thaw cycles vs 0 in snow-covered plots over winter; soil temperature ranged −3.6 to 4.2°C (SF) vs −0.2 to 0.8°C (SC) at 10 cm depth.
- Winter stem shrinkage (PLDwinter) was significantly higher in SF (8.6% for *Acer* to 13.9% for *Sorbus*) than SC (−1.5% to 2.1%; P < 0.001).
- Spring hydraulic conductivity loss (PLCspring) averaged 77.8% (SF) vs 31.3% (SC) (P < 0.001); *Sorbus*, *Picea* and *Larix* exceeded 80% loss under SF.
- PLCspring positively correlated with PLDwinter (r²=0.291) and with PLDspring (r²=0.256/0.476 depending on comparison).
- Spring glucose and fructose contents were roughly twofold higher under SF than SC; no significant difference in total NSC or starch.
- Relative diameter growth (RDG) was reduced under SF for all species, significantly so for *Picea* (43.0% SC vs 4.5% SF) and *Sorbus* (11.9% vs −1.9%).
- Summer mortality index (MIsummer) was significantly higher under SF for all species (P < 0.001); *Larix* and *Juniperus* were most affected in SF, *Larix* also showed high mortality even in SC (no apparent recovery strategy for this species).
- MIsummer positively correlated with PLCspring, PLDspring, spring glucose and fructose; negatively with spring sucrose and starch; no correlation with total NSC.
- No direct correlation was found between winter diameter loss (PLDwinter) and either summer growth (RDG, P=0.101) or a species/growth-form-based model — physiological variables were much better predictors of mortality than snow-cover exposure or growth form alone.

## Advantages and Limitations

- Small sample size per species/treatment (n=3–10 trees per species per treatment for many measurements, n=39 sensors total across 5 species x 2 treatments) — limits statistical power for species-level significance, and several species-level contrasts were reported as non-significant despite visible trends (explicitly noted by the authors as due to high inter-individual variability).
- Single site, single elevation (1700 m), single winter season of snow manipulation (2021–2022) — results may not generalize across snowpack regimes, elevations, or interannual climate variability; the authors themselves flag that cumulative effects of successive snow-free winters (not tested here) could matter more than a single event.
- Correlational, not causal, links between physiological variables (PLC, PLD, NSC) and mortality/growth — the "best predictive model" is a statistical association from a small dataset, not a mechanistically validated causal test.
- Saplings were nursery-grown for one summer before planting and transplanted, which may not fully represent natural regeneration/establishment dynamics at treeline.
- Strength: multi-method physiological assessment (HRCT embolism imaging + electrolyte leakage + carbohydrate assays + continuous dendrometry) applied consistently across 5 functionally diverse species is unusually thorough for a single field experiment, and the explicit resistance/recovery framing is conceptually useful.
- The study is squarely about saplings/shrubs, not the treeline position or adult tree distribution itself — relevant as a mechanistic underpinning for treeline shift studies, not a direct study of treeline advance/retreat.

## Conclusion

- Loss of snow cover at the alpine treeline exposes saplings to significantly more hydraulic damage, living-cell mortality and summer mortality, and reduces growth, compared to snow-covered conditions.
- Species differ substantially in resistance and recovery mechanisms: some species avoid damage (*Picea* via low PLC, refilling), others tolerate damage and recover via resprouting (*Sorbus*, *Acer*) or maintain growth but with no recovery option (*Larix*, most vulnerable overall; *Juniperus*, most sensitive to snow removal specifically).
- Mortality risk was best predicted by combining spring hydraulic loss, spring diameter loss (cell mortality), and spring sucrose/starch content — reflecting a joint effect of hydraulic failure, cell mortality and carbon allocation disruption.
- Under climate-change scenarios of shorter, thinner snow cover, survival and regeneration of young trees and shrubs at high elevation is likely to be jeopardized, though species-specific recovery strategies could buffer this to varying degrees.
