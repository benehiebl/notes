## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Observed snow depth trends in the European Alps: 1971 to 2019"
- Michael Matiu, Alice Crespi, Giacomo Bertoldi, Carlo Maria Carmagnola, Christoph Marty, Samuel Morin, Wolfgang Schöner and further co-authors (long author list; only the first are given here)
- *The Cryosphere* 15: 1343–1382 (2021); doi: 10.5194/tc-15-1343-2021

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** Snow cover controls Alpine hydrology, ecology and treeline processes (insulation, growing-season length), but earlier station analyses were regional.
- **What was done?** Daily snow depth from more than 2000 stations in six Alpine countries (Austria, France, Germany, Italy, Slovenia, Switzerland) was compiled, regionalised with PCA and k-means, and analysed for trends (1971–2019, over 800 stations) in monthly and seasonal snow depth and snow cover duration.
- **What is the main outcome?** Snow depth decreased at most stations from November to May: −8.4 % per decade for seasonal mean snow depth, −5.6 % for seasonal maximum and −5.6 % for snow cover duration; trends were strongest in spring and at low elevations where snow turns to snow-free, and differed by region at the same elevation.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: provide an Alpine-wide, consistent picture of how snow depth varies in space and changed over 1971–2019.
- Concept: snow depth responds to temperature (negative correlation) and precipitation (positive), with the snow–albedo feedback amplifying change at the snow/no-snow transition; regional climate gradients (north-south and west-east) mean that results cannot be generalised from one region.

## Technical Approach

- **Data**: daily snow depth (HS) from national weather services and other providers; stations with gap filling; station counts rising from about 1900, with jumps in the 1960s–1980s and a sharp decline after 2017 because Austrian data were only available until then.
- **Regionalisation**: PCA of daily snow-depth series (first five PCs explain most of the variance; additional PCs <2.6 %) followed by k-means with elbow method and silhouette coefficients, yielding five regions: north and high Alpine, north-east, north-west, south-east, south and high Alpine.
- **Climate relations**: station correlations with temperature and precipitation from the MESCAN-SURFEX reanalysis (temperature lapse-rate adjusted with 6.5 °C/km), tested against observation-based grids.
- **Trends**: Theil–Sen slopes with Mann–Kendall significance on monthly mean snow depth, seasonal mean/maximum snow depth and snow cover duration (November–May); compared with GLS and negative-binomial models (negligible differences); elevation bands of 500 or 1000 m.

## Distinctive Features

- First Alpine-wide in situ assessment of snow depth trends covering six countries (as claimed by the authors).
- Data-driven regions that match climatic forcing zones rather than administrative ones.
- Explicit attention to elevation- and region-dependent trends and to snow-albedo feedback signals.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- 85 % of station–month combinations (November–May) showed decreasing mean snow depth, of which 26 % were significant (p < 0.05); 15 % increased, of which fewer than 1 % were significant.
- Stronger and more significant negative trends in spring (April–May) and, at low elevations, throughout the season; snow cover duration decreased below 2000 m, and above that no consistent change was found, partly due to the choice of snow season (November–May).
- Seasonal maximum snow depth decreased more in absolute terms than seasonal mean, but less in relative terms.
- Decreases in the south were larger than in the north, and relative decreases even more so because snow depths are lower in the south; NE stations at times showed increases in some months (e.g. February).
- Temperature correlations strongly negative below about 1000 m (−0.8 to −0.5) and weakening with elevation (about −0.2 up to 2000 m); precipitation correlations were positive and increased mildly with elevation.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Large, multi-national station dataset with documented regionalisation.
- Multiple trend estimators agree; sensitivity analyses reported.

**Limitations**
- Series were not homogenised (no common framework possible); snow cover duration and maximum depth are less affected by inhomogeneities, but trends in mean depth may contain non-climatic breaks.
- Station elevation distribution is skewed to low elevations (maximum at about 500 m), few stations above 2000 m (none above 3200 m), so conclusions for the treeline zone and above are uncertain, particularly in the north.
- Many series have a data gap in 1981–1997 and the number of stations drops after 2017.
- Daily snow depth reflects local conditions (wind redistribution, small-scale topography), so station trends are not necessarily representative of the forest-line environment.
- The study quantifies trends, not their ecological effect.

## Conclusion

- Alpine snow depth and duration declined since 1971, mostly at low and mid elevations in spring, with regional heterogeneity.
