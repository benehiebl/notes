## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Frost resistance in alpine woody plants"
- Gilbert Neuner (University of Innsbruck)
- *Frontiers in Plant Science* 5: 654 (2014); doi: 10.3389/fpls.2014.00654

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** Frost is a core hypothesis for the upper limit of trees and for the effect of reduced snow cover under warming, and this review gives the physiological basis and measured thresholds.
- **What was done?** A review of frost resistance and frost survival mechanisms (tolerance, avoidance by supercooling, escape) of leaves, stems, buds, flowers and fruits of alpine woody plants, with a survey table of 15 species and infrared-thermography evidence of freezing processes.
- **What is the main outcome?** Winter frost damage risk is low in alpine woody plants, but spring and summer frosts can damage them, and premature deacclimation (notably *Pinus cembra*) and loss of snow cover in spring may become critical under climate change.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: summarise frost resistance and frost survival mechanisms in alpine woody plants and point out future research areas.
- Concept: freezing temperatures occur throughout the growing season in the alpine life zone; frost severity (intensity and duration) increases with elevation; small woody plants are insulated by snow while trees are exposed.
- Frost resistance is defined by tolerance of extracellular freezing and dehydration, by supercooling (avoidance) or by escape through life cycle timing.

## Technical Approach

- Narrative literature review, with emphasis on the author's own group's work on experimental freezing tests, LT50/LT10 values and high-resolution infrared thermography.
- Compares organs and tissues: mature leaves, developing leaves and shoots, resting vegetative buds, reproductive buds, flowers and fruits, and xylem parenchyma cells.
- Compares trees with woody shrubs, and exposed with snow-covered plants, and summarises frost resistance at different elevations (600 m vs 1950 m; Table 3).

## Distinctive Features

- Organ-by-organ and mechanism-by-mechanism synthesis for alpine woody species.
- Visualises ice nucleation, propagation and supercooling with infrared thermography.
- Explicitly links snow cover, acclimation state and climate-change-related deacclimation risk.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- Treeline-height trees can be up to 9.3 K colder than air temperature at night (earlier reports 3–8 K), so frost exposure is higher than air temperature suggests.
- Summer frost: reproductive shoots are the most susceptible (about −4.6 °C), followed by developing leaves/shoots; damage thresholds for developing shoots were reported at −3.4 °C (*Rhododendron ferrugineum*), −4.4 °C (*Picea abies*), −4.8 °C (*Pinus cembra*), −5.5 °C (*Sorbus aucuparia*) and −6.3 °C (*Larix decidua*), temperatures that can occur in the treeline ecotone during the growing season.
- Midwinter frost resistance of mature leaves of trees and shrubs ranges from about −26 to −58.5 °C when naturally acclimated; resting buds of conifers are generally more resistant, so winter frost damage risk is low.
- Snow-covered shrubs keep temperatures near 0 °C and have less frost hardening than exposed plants; during mild winters with early snow melt, plants do not harden fully and then lose snow cover, which makes them vulnerable.
- Experimental evidence: premature deacclimation of *P. cembra* in late winter (induced by artificial warming) and limited reacclimation ability of many alpine shrubs are potential problems under projected climate.
- Supercooling of reproductive buds and some tissues down to about −25 to −30 °C or deeper (xylem parenchyma down to −40 °C or below in some species), with failure when temperatures exceed the supercooling capacity.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Consolidates scattered physiological data and gives threshold values.
- Clear separation of risks by season and organ.

**Limitations**
- Narrative review without systematic search; relies heavily on a few species and on the author's own, partly unpublished data (e.g. some table values).
- Mostly short-term laboratory freezing tests; field survival and demography are not quantified.
- Statements on climate-change effects (premature deacclimation, spring frost) are projections and based on few experiments, mostly on *P. cembra*.
- No treeline-scale or remote-sensing link.

## Conclusion

- Alpine woody plants are well adapted to winter cold, but spring frost after early deacclimation or snow loss is the key physiological risk.
- Supports the snow-cover/frost hypothesis tested experimentally in [[charra_2025_snow_treeline]] and the concept [[snow_cover_treeline]]; see also [[wieser_2019_effects_climate_treeline]], [[matiu_2021_snow_depth_trends]], [[treeline_ecotone_theory]].
