# Alessi et al. 2019 — Ancient Refugia and Present-day Habitat Suitability of Native Laurophylls in Italy

## Title and Authors
**Ancient refugia and present-day habitat suitability of native laurophylls in Italy**
Nicola Alessi, Jakub Tešitel, Stefan Zerbe, Francesco Spada, Emiliano Agrillo, Camilla Wellstein — *Journal of Vegetation Science* (accepted article, DOI: 10.1111/jvs.12743, ~2019).

## Quick Overview
- **Why is it relevant?** Provides the first national-scale quantitative analysis of native Italian laurophyllous (EVE broadleaved) species distribution relative to their potential range — directly underpins any interpretation of present-day EVE cover maps and future RS monitoring targets.
- **What was done?** Used 17,087 Italian forest vegetation plots to identify 11 native laurophyllous species via DCA ordination, then estimated potential ranges with Beals' sociological favourability index, and mapped realized vs potential aggregations across Italy.
- **What is the main outcome?** Most Italian native laurophylls occupy **< 50% of their potential range**, indicating non-equilibrium with current climate — their distribution is better explained by dispersal limitation from Quaternary refugia in the **central Apennines** than by ongoing climate change. Italian forests show high potential for future EVE expansion throughout the Apennines and southern Alps.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept
European laurophyllous vegetation (warm, humid, shade-tolerant evergreen broad-leaved species of Tertiary origin) was largely extirpated during Quaternary cooling events. A small subset of native Italian laurophylls persisted in Quaternary refugia and is now potentially re-expanding. The paper asks: are native Italian laurophylls in equilibrium with today's climate, where are their ancient refugia, and where are their available expansion areas?

The distinction from the widely studied *introduced* EVE species (*Prunus laurocerasus*, *Trachycarpus fortunei*) is central — this paper focuses exclusively on **native** laurophylls whose spread reflects biogeographical history rather than only recent propagule pressure from gardens.

## Technical Approach
- **Data**: 17,087 Italian forest vegetation plots from the "Vegetation Plots Database – La Sapienza" (Agrillo et al. 2017)
- **Laurophyll identification**: DCA ordination with climate and EIV overlays; selected all native broadleaved evergreen phanerophytes with ≥50 occurrences; applied biogeographic filter excluding Arcto-Tertiary and Quaternary lineages; identified laurophylls as those with humid + warm DCA centroid positions (threshold: DCA score of *Laurus nobilis*)
- **Two subgroups** derived by splitting within the laurophyll cluster: mesothermic (ME) and macrothermic (MA)
- **Potential range**: Beals' index of sociological favourability — estimates probability of occurrence for each species in unoccupied plots using species co-occurrence patterns in the full database; threshold at 10th percentile of occupied sites
- **Equilibrium assessment**: realized/potential range-size ratio for each species
- **Aggregation mapping**: count of co-occurring laurophylls within 0.1 decimal degree radius of each plot; IDW interpolation at 5 km resolution for both realized and potential aggregations

## Distinctive Features
- **Sociological-niche approach via Beals' index**: uses species co-occurrences as an integrator of both abiotic conditions and biotic interactions rather than using climate-only habitat models — captures fine-scale ecological context
- **Biogeographic lineage filter**: rigorously distinguishes Tertiary paleotropical laurophylls from Mediterranean sclerophylls and from introduced ornamental species — identifies the specifically Italian *native* laurophyll pool
- **Realized vs potential aggregation maps**: visualises both ancient refugia (core of realized richness) and future expansion potential (unoccupied suitable sites)

## Experimental Setup and Results

**11 Italian native laurophylls identified:**

| Subgroup | Species |
|---|---|
| ME (mesothermic) | *Daphne laureola*, *Ilex aquifolium*, *Ruscus hypoglossum*, *Hypericum androsaemum*, *Buxus sempervirens* |
| MA (macrothermic) | *Hedera helix*, *Hypericum hircinum*, *Pyracantha coccinea*, *Lonicera etrusca*, *Ruscus aculeatus*, *Laurus nobilis* |

ME occupied humid, shaded, cool sites; MA occupied drier, sunnier, warmer sites.

**Range equilibrium:**
- **9 of 11 species** occupied < 50% of their potential range
- Only *Hedera helix* and *Ruscus aculeatus* exceeded 60% occupancy
- **Non-equilibrium with current climate** is the rule, not the exception

**Spatial distribution:**
- **Realized aggregations**: core in the **central Apennines** for both ME and MA; MA extends slightly southward to Sicily
- **Potential aggregations**: much more extensive; entire Apennines + pre-Alps + Sicily + Sardinia are suitable; ME favours Apenninic ridges; MA shows higher coastal potentiality
- **Insubrian region** (where laurophyllisation was first described) is highlighted as highly suitable for ME, independent of current climate warming

**Why non-equilibrium?**
1. **Post-glacial dispersal limitation**: European tree species are known to underoccupy suitable sites due to insufficient post-glacial recolonization from southern refugia
2. **Human forest clearance**: native laurophylls are late-successional species requiring mature closed-canopy forest — their spread was limited by centuries of anthropogenic forest clearance in Mediterranean countries
3. **Climate change as secondary driver**: the Insubrian pre-Alps were already highly suitable for native ME laurophylls before recent warming; climate warming accelerates expansion but was not the trigger

**Implications for EVE expansion:**
- Land-use abandonment + forest ageing → increasing suitable niches for late-successional EVE species
- Available niches exist throughout the Apennines, Insubrian region, Sicily, Sardinia
- Future spread will be facilitated by both increased propagule pressure and suitable microhabitat availability

## Advantages and Limitations
- **Advantages**: 17,087 plots provide unprecedented national coverage; Beals' index integrates biotic interactions; species-specific range-size ratios quantify non-equilibrium explicitly; realized aggregation maps reveal refugia directly from empirical data.
- **Limitations**: Database biases — Insubrian region underrepresented in sample plots (may underestimate observed aggregations in the pre-Alps); plot-scale resolution; Beals' index not equivalent to mechanistic habitat modelling; snapshot in time (no temporal trend analysis).

## Conclusion
**Native Italian laurophylls are not in equilibrium with current climate — they are underexpanding relative to available suitable habitat.** The central Apennines harboured Quaternary refugia for these species and remain the richest realized aggregations. Italian forests show widespread potential for EVE expansion driven primarily by dispersal limitation and land-use change, with climate playing a secondary amplifying role. This reframes EVE mapping objectives: the question is not just *where EVE currently is* but *where it is headed given available potential habitat* — making realized and potential aggregation maps complementary products for any RS-based EVE monitoring effort.
