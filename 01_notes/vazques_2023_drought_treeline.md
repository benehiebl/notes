## Title and Authors of the Paper

Snow, fire and drought: how alpine and treeline soil seed banks are affected by simulated climate change — Jerónimo Vázquez-Ramírez and Susanna E. Venn (Centre for Integrative Ecology, Deakin University). Published in *Annals of Botany* 135: 223–238 (received 26 May 2023, accepted 23 November 2023).

## Quick Overview
- **Why is it relevant?** It shows how three climate-change-linked stressors (reduced snow cover, fire, spring/summer drought) affect seed persistence and germination in an alpine treeline ecotone versus alpine herbfield, i.e. whether the *soil seed bank* can support future recruitment as climate changes.
- **What was done?** Soil cores were collected from Eucalyptus pauciflora treeline and alpine herbfield sites in Kosciuszko National Park (Australia), and subjected to a full factorial greenhouse germination experiment with 8 treatment combinations (snow reduction, fire simulation, drought, and their combinations).
- **What is the main outcome?** Drought had the strongest, most consistent negative effect on germinant number and diversity in both communities; snow reduction reduced herbfield (but not treeline) germinants; fire had a neutral effect on total germinant numbers but shifted composition toward shrubs; seed banks were consistently and strongly compositionally dissimilar from the standing vegetation.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept
- Test how three simulated climate-change events — reduced snow cover (→ shorter cold stratification + more soil freeze–thaw cycles), fire (heat + smoke), and spring/summer drought — affect the density, diversity and species composition of germinants from soil seed banks.
- Compare two high-elevation plant communities that differ in their relationship to future warming: the tall alpine herbfield (the most widespread Australian alpine community) and the alpine treeline ecotone (dominated by the single tree species *Eucalyptus pauciflora*, expected to migrate upslope under warming).
- Underlying concept: soil seed banks are a "storage solution" and recruitment buffer for plant populations, but their response to climate change has been understudied relative to above-ground vegetation responses.

## Technical Approach
- Study area: Kosciuszko National Park, Snowy Mountains, Australia; 3 paired sites, each with 50 m² plots for herbfield (~1950 m a.s.l.) and treeline (~1800 m a.s.l.), soil cores (240 per community) collected in autumn 2020/2021.
- Seedling emergence method (standard for soil seed bank studies): soil spread in trays, germinants identified to species and removed/counted weekly for 30 weeks (20 weeks of watering treatment + 10-week post-disturbance follow-up).
- Full factorial design (8 treatments): Control (C), Snow reduction (S), Fire (F), Drought (D), and all combinations (SF, SD, FD, SFD).
  - Snow reduction = shortened cold stratification (10 → 6 weeks at 2 °C) plus simulated freeze–thaw events (−5 °C pulses).
  - Fire = oven heat (80 °C, 5 min) + commercial smoke-water application.
  - Drought = reduced soil volumetric water content (25–30 % vs 40–45 % control) for 20 weeks during the simulated spring/summer growing period.
- Analysis: generalized linear mixed models (Poisson/negative binomial) on cumulative germinant counts; Hill-number diversity profiles; NMDS + ANOSIM/SIMPER to compare seed bank vs standing vegetation composition; functional-trait grouping (life form, regeneration strategy, seed mass, dispersal distance) to explain treatment responses.

## Distinctive Features
- One of the first studies to test *combined/factorial* climate-change stressors (not just single-factor warming) on high-elevation soil seed banks, explicitly targeting the treeline ecotone rather than only alpine vegetation.
- Uses a heterogeneous, naturally accumulated field soil seed bank (not freshly collected seeds from parent plants), which the authors argue is more realistic than most prior single-species/fresh-seed germination studies.
- Explicitly quantifies seed bank–vegetation dissimilarity (via NMDS/ANOSIM) as an ecological outcome, not just germinant counts.
- Traits framework (life form, regeneration strategy, seed mass, dispersal distance) used to interpret *why* certain treatments affected germination, revealing that graminoids and short-dispersal-distance species were consistently most negatively affected, while seed mass explained little.

## Experimental Setup and Results
- Seed bank density (control trays): herbfield 32.86 ± 3.61 seeds L⁻¹ (~3277 seeds m⁻²); treeline 45.66 ± 4.97 seeds L⁻¹ (~4561 seeds m⁻²) — treeline seed bank was larger and more diverse (37 species/19 families vs 28 species/15 families).
- Snow reduction: significant negative effect on herbfield germinant number (fewer, driven by species with strong cold-stratification requirements, e.g. *Wahlenbergia ceracea*, *Oreomyrrhis eriopoda*); treeline germinants largely unaffected.
- Fire: no significant effect on total germinant number in either community; increased shrub germinants (e.g. *Epacris glacialis*, *Kunzea muelleri*, *Baeckea utilis*) and decreased forb abundance/evenness; no effect on the sole tree species (*Eucalyptus pauciflora*) germination.
- Drought: strongest and most consistent negative effect — reduced germinant number by 72 % in the herbfield vs 44 % in the treeline (relative to control), reduced richness/evenness, delayed germination timing (most germinants emerged only after the drought period ended, indicating a "carry-over"/secondary dormancy effect), and its effect statistically overrode other treatments in factorial combinations.
- Seed bank vs standing vegetation: both communities showed high dissimilarity (herbfield R = 0.746; treeline R = 0.857, ANOSIM); only 14 species (50 % of herbfield total, 37.8 % of treeline total) were shared between the two communities' seed banks. No evidence found of treeline species migrating into the herbfield soil seed bank.
- Treatments generally *increased* dissimilarity between germinants and standing vegetation further (e.g. snow reduction + fire and snow reduction + drought raised herbfield R from 0.719 to ~0.85).

## Advantages and Limitations
- Single geographic region (Kosciuszko National Park, Australian Alps) with only 3 paired sites per community — limited spatial replication and unclear generalizability to other alpine/treeline systems worldwide, especially Northern Hemisphere conifer treelines.
- Single tree species at the treeline (*Eucalyptus pauciflora*); conclusions about "treeline seed bank resilience" cannot be extended to multi-species treeline ecotones.
- Greenhouse conditions were warmer (day/night mean 22.8/17.6 °C) than in-situ alpine soil surface temperatures, which the authors themselves flag as likely biasing results toward *lower* germination and possibly higher apparent richness than would occur under true field warming — an acknowledged methodological caveat.
- Seedling emergence method is known to underestimate total seed bank size and may fail to break dormancy of some species, so absolute density/diversity values are likely conservative.
- Single simulated fire event and single drought intensity/timing — real fire regimes and drought severity/frequency vary; results describe one scenario, not a range of possible future intensities.
- Soil was sampled once (autumn 2020/2021), so seasonal/interannual seed bank dynamics (transient vs persistent fractions) are not captured — the authors note this as a gap for future work.
- Study measures germination potential in a controlled setting, not actual field recruitment/seedling survival to maturity — so it is a proxy for, not a direct measure of, future population-level recruitment success under climate change.
- Strengths: full factorial design (rare in this literature, which mostly tests single stressors), realistic field-collected heterogeneous seed bank, robust statistical treatment (GLMMs, Hill diversity profiles, multivariate community comparisons), and direct paired comparison of two ecologically contrasting communities using identical methodology.

## Conclusion
- Simulated climate change (reduced snow, fire, drought) has neutral-to-negative effects on soil seed bank germination in both alpine herbfield and treeline communities, with drought consistently the dominant and most damaging stressor.
- Snow reduction disproportionately harmed the herbfield (via loss of cold-stratification benefits), while the treeline seed bank was comparatively more robust to snow loss but equally vulnerable to drought.
- Fire did not reduce total germinant numbers but shifted community composition toward shrubs, plausibly reinforcing shrub-fire feedback loops reported elsewhere in alpine Australia.
- Soil seed banks are compositionally very different from standing vegetation in both communities, and climate treatments tend to increase this dissimilarity — meaning seed banks may become a progressively less reliable predictor of future above-ground community composition.
- The authors conclude soil seed banks will likely continue to contribute to alpine/treeline plant recruitment under climate change, but less so than under current conditions, and with probable shifts in future community composition.
