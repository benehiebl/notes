## Title and Authors of the Paper

- "Phenological and elevational shifts of plants, animals and fungi under climate change in the European Alps"
- Yann Vitasse, Sylvain Ursenbacher, Geoffrey Klein, Thierry Bohnenstengel, Yannick Chittaro, Anne Delestrade, Christian Monnerat, Martine Rebetez, Christian Rixen, Nicolas Strebel, Benedikt R. Schmidt, Sonja Wipf, Thomas Wohlgemuth, Nigel Gilles Yoccoz, Jonathan Lenoir
- *Biological Reviews* (2021); doi: 10.1111/brv.12727

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** It quantifies how fast organisms in the Alps respond to a warming of about 0.36 °C per decade since 1970 and compares this with the speed of isotherm shifts, which frames treeline and woody-plant shift rates.
- **What was done?** A quantitative review of published and previously unpublished data on spring phenology and elevational range shifts of plants, animals and fungi (N = 2133 species for range shifts) in the European Alps.
- **What is the main outcome?** Spring phenology shifted earlier (leaf-out of woody plants −2.4 days per decade) and optimum elevations shifted upslope by +18 to +25 m per decade on average (woody plants +32.7 m per decade), which is clearly slower than isotherm shifts of about +62 to +71 m per decade since 1970.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

- Goal: synthesise climate-related changes in spring phenology and elevational distribution across taxa in the Alps.
- Concept: if species track climate, range shifts should keep pace with isotherm velocity; a slower shift implies disequilibrium (climatic debt) and potential phenological and spatial mismatches among interacting species.
- The Alps offer short distances between isotherms, but species near summits lose habitat area when moving upward.

## Technical Approach

- **Search**: literature search for studies reporting either elevational range shifts or spring phenological trends over recent decades, plus previously unpublished long-term data sets; data summarised in Tables 1 and 2.
- **Metrics**: phenology (first activity of animals, leaf-out and flowering of plants, in days per decade and days per °C); elevational shifts as change in optimum elevation (maximum abundance), upper range limit (leading edge) and lower limit, in m per decade.
- **Synthesis**: climate change in the Alps (temperature, snow cover, glacier retreat), phenological shifts, range shifts, plant–animal interactions and research gaps.

## Distinctive Features

- Covers many taxa (plants, reptiles, insects, birds, amphibians, fungi) with a common metric.
- Compares biological shifts directly with the velocity of isotherms.
- Combines published results with unpublished monitoring data.

## Experimental Setup and Results

- Climate: air temperature increase about 0.36 °C per decade since 1970, glacier retreat and snowpack reduction.
- Phenology: first spring activity of reptiles and terrestrial insects shifted earlier by −5.7 and −6.0 days per decade; leaf-out and flowering of woody and herbaceous plants −2.4 and −2.8 days per decade; amphibians, resident bird singing/laying and semi-aquatic insects showed small non-significant trends (−1.0 to +1.3 days per decade). Woody plants advanced leaf-out by about −5.1 ± 3.9 days per °C.
- Elevation of optimum: +18 to +25 m per decade on average across taxa; +36.2 m per decade for terrestrial insects and +32.7 m per decade for woody plants; between −1.0 and +11 m per decade for semi-aquatic insects, ferns, birds and wood-decaying fungi.
- Upper range limit: animals +47 to +91 m per decade, plants +17 to +40 m per decade.
- Woody plants: the shift of long-lived woody plants is at least as fast as that of non-alpine herbaceous plants; the authors note that forest-only studies may underestimate shifts because they miss the zone beyond the forest limit, and that seedlings and saplings already occur above adult limits. They also note that a large part of treeline shifts has been attributed to land abandonment (Swiss and Austrian Alps) and reduced grazing (Italian Alps).
- Except possibly for terrestrial insects, shifts are too slow to keep pace with isotherm shifts.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**
- Broad taxonomic synthesis that includes unpublished data.
- Explicit comparison between biological and climatic velocity.

**Limitations**
- Heterogeneous study designs, periods and methods (resurveys, monitoring, citizen science), so rates are not strictly comparable; sampling effort and detection biases are not corrected.
- Uneven data coverage by taxon and region, and few studies on trees at the treeline.
- Attribution to climate is correlative; land use, precipitation and post-Little-Ice-Age recolonisation also act (acknowledged by the authors).
- No treeline-specific quantification, so it provides a context for rates, not for treeline positions.

## Conclusion

- Phenology and elevational ranges are shifting in the expected direction across Alpine taxa, but organisms are lagging behind isotherm shifts.
