## 1. Definitions (forest line, timberline, tree species line, treeline) and consequences for measured shifts

### Takeaway
Boundary definition determines the measured rate: the forest line/timberline (closed forest), the tree line (upright trees >~2-3 m) and the tree species line (uppermost individual of a tree species) move at different speeds and respond to different drivers. The newest Italian Alps data show timberline moving 1.6 m/yr versus 1.1 m/yr for the tree species line (preprint), and Holocene data show timberline and treeline separated by ~100+ m with different fluctuation amplitudes.

## 2. Observed shift, advance and stagnation rates in the Alps by region and method

### Takeaway
Reported Alpine rates span roughly 0.3 to >1.6 m/yr and are strongly method- and site-dependent. Photo-based Italian studies show the highest values; Landsat for South Tyrol gives ~1 m/yr; Swiss field resurvey ~1 m/yr. No peer-reviewed quantitative treeline-position shift for the Austrian side of Tyrol was found (only physiology, growth, modelling and vegetation-change studies).

## 3. Land-use legacy, anthropogenic treeline depression, Holocene treeline history (potential vs actual treeline)

### Takeaway
Palaeo-records show the Alpine timberline was 100-180 m higher during the early/mid-Holocene, and that fire plus human land use over the last ~4000 years changed species composition at the treeline (loss of Larix/P. cembra, expansion of dwarf shrub/open land). Present-day forest lines in much of the Alps lie below the climatic potential because of grazing/clearing history, so current advance is partly recovery. Whether today's treeless alpine zone is "natural" (Tinner & Theurillat) or largely anthropogenic is contested.

### Inferences

## 4. Time lags and disequilibrium between treeline and climate

### Takeaway
Evidence for lag is mostly qualitative or indirect: Körner (2024 conference abstract) reports Eastern Tyrol P. cembra lagging the isotherm shift; modelling shows dispersal/recruitment limitation; palaeo-data show multi-century responses. A quantitative, peer-reviewed lag estimate for Alpine treelines was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Körner C. (2024) EGU abstract, DOI 10.5194/egusphere-egu24-20484: see prior notes (Eastern Tyrol P. cembra lags the ~2 K warming isotherm shift; conference abstract only).
- Dullinger S., Dirnböck T., Grabherr G. (2004) J. Ecol. 92(2): 241-252, DOI 10.1111/j.0022-0477.2004.00872.x: VERIFIED in prior notes (Austrian Northern Calcareous Alps, P. mugo; dispersal and invasibility slow the response).

### Inferences
- Because the actual forest line is below the potential line in many Alpine regions (Q3), "disequilibrium" has two components: land-use (lag from historic depression) and demographic (generation time of P. cembra/Larix, dispersal). Studies rarely separate them; avoid describing the entire gap as climate lag.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed Pellissier treeline-lag paper; no direct estimate of generation-time-based lag; Körner 2024 awaits peer review.

## 5. Regional climate warming and snow trends in the Alps (temperature, precipitation, snow cover, drought at treeline elevations)

### Takeaway
The Greater Alpine Region warmed ~1.2 C in the 20th century with the strongest step after the 1970s; Alpine warming rate since 1970 is ~0.36 C/decade (Vitasse 2021 citing the literature). Snow depth fell at 87% of >2000 stations (1971-2019), with larger spring losses at 1000-3000 m; in Austria seasonal snow cover duration fell ~42 days on average since 1961. Treeline-elevation (1800-2400 m) trends for snow are weaker and more heterogeneous than at mid elevations. Drought trends at the treeline elevation remain poorly documented.

### Inferences
- The elevation-dependence of the snow signal matters for treeline stress: losses are largest in spring and at mid-to-high elevations (Matiu 2021) while the Austria-wide relative decline is smaller at the highest elevations (Olefs 2020); a treeline ecotone at 2000-2300 m therefore sits where snow-cover shortening and early snowmelt both operate, potentially exposing saplings to frost/desiccation (Charra-Vaskou 2026, prior notes).
- Observed warming is solid; warming-driven drought at treeline elevation is a projection (Gobiet 2014), not an observed trend in the verified literature.

## 6. Evidence for stagnation or retreat (drought, frost, snow, wind, avalanche, pathogens, browsing) in the Alps

### Takeaway
Direct documentation of retreat at the Alpine treeline is rare; stagnation is explained by multi-factor limitations: frost drought/winter desiccation, snow fungi, avalanches/geomorphology, wind/Foehn, dispersal limitation and grazing. No verified Alpine study shows drought-induced treeline retreat (confirms prior notes).

### Inferences
- The strongest evidence of "stress" in the Tyrolean treeline ecotone is physiological (winter frost drought, snow fungi, reduced snow cover exposure) rather than population-level retreat. For a satellite-based analysis, stagnation can only be attributed to drought after excluding slope-aspect, wind, land-use and disturbance factors.
- Contradiction to flag: Harsch 2009 (1% recession) and Carrieri preprint (advance at all nine sites) versus physiological studies describing mortality mechanisms. These are not necessarily in conflict because mortality is local and timing-dependent, but no study scales physiological stress to boundary shifts.

### Gaps
