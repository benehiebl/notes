## 1. Snow as insulation vs hazard: snow cover trends, snow manipulation, snow fungi, snowmelt timing, seedling vs adult, krummholz

### Takeaway
Alpine snow cover is declining (about -5.6 % per decade in snow cover duration, 1971-2019), mainly through earlier melt and mostly below high elevation, but at treeline the evidence says snow is a double-edged factor: too little snow exposes saplings to winter dehydration (Charra-Vaskou 2026), while long-lasting snow raises fungal and mortality risk (Barbeito 2012, 2013), and late snowmelt is the single strongest predictor of planted-conifer mortality. Direct snow-manipulation evidence for adult treeline conifers, and any evidence for P. cembra saplings under reduced snow, is missing.

### Inferences
- The picture is contextual, not "less snow bad": winter-protective snow (Charra-Vaskou 2026, saplings below treeline) and snowmelt-date mortality (Barbeito 2012, Larix/P. mugo/P. cembra plantation) pull in opposite directions; both can be true at different microsites. Where melt is early, spring radiation/frost stress after emergence (Bader 2018) is the plausible mechanism; where melt is very late, fungi and shortened growing season (Barbeito 2012, 2013).
- For adult trees above the snowpack, snow trend declines matter mainly through soil frost, spring phenology and drought timing; no adult-tree evidence retrieved.

## 2. Frost drought, winter desiccation, freeze-thaw embolism and refilling; soil frost and root uptake

### Takeaway
Timberline conifers in the Alps experience winter xylem embolism through the combination of drought (frost drought: low water potential when soil water is frozen) and repeated freeze-thaw events; P. abies is vulnerable and refills in late winter, while P. cembra at the same timberline showed no embolism in the 2003 study. Larix is deciduous and this stress is mostly a Picea/P. cembra topic. Soil frost and root uptake are the weakest-documented part in the Alps.

### Cited Findings

- Mayr S, Schwienbacher F, Bauer H (2003) Winter at the alpine timberline. Why does embolism occur in Norway spruce but not in stone pine? Plant Physiology 131(2): 780-792. DOI 10.1104/pp.011452. Abstract read. System: Picea abies and Pinus cembra at 1700 m and 2100 m (Central Alps), two winters. ### Inferences
- The only verified direct evidence that frozen soil limits water supply to timberline conifers is indirect: Mayr 2014 states refilling started when the soil was still frozen and soil water unavailable, implying uptake via branches rather than roots (Picea).
- The P. cembra vs P. abies difference (Mayr 2003) is a single timberline comparison; whether P. cembra is generally resistant, or only avoided embolism in those winters, is not settled (the 2003 abstract gives avoidance as the hypothesis).
- Larix decidua is leafless in winter, so winter desiccation of needles is not applicable; winter stress for Larix is bud/shoot desiccation and sapling-level xylem effects (Charra-Vaskou 2026 includes Larix saplings).

## 3. Frost: freezing resistance, dehardening, spring and late frost, frost rings

### Takeaway
Timberline conifers harden to very low winter temperatures, so absolute winter minima are unlikely to be damaging; the risk window is spring dehardening and flushing. For deciduous trees at their upper limit this safety margin is constant with elevation (Lenz 2013); direct evidence for P. cembra dehardening exists (Buchner and Neuner 2011, metadata only), and for Larix flush frost damage I found none.

## 4. Summer drought and heat at treeline: evidence, hydraulic safety, NSC, carry-over and legacy effects

### Takeaway
Verified evidence shows water stress limits larch (and to a lesser extent P. cembra) in dry inner-Alpine valleys up to about 1700-2000 m, but at the treeline itself saplings of Picea and Larix showed no sap-flow response to top-soil drought (Wieser 2019), and treeline-elevation drought evidence for adult P. cembra is not verified. Legacy effects of drought are well documented globally, but not at the Alpine treeline.

Extrapolation to treeline (about 2000-2300 m) is not supported by what was verified; Wieser 2019 argues against it for saplings.
- The sequential drought leading to winter stress (or winter embolism leading to summer mortality) link is documented only by Charra-Vaskou 2026 (saplings) and, for spruce legacy, Knüver 2022 (lowland experiment).
- Because Dietrich 2018/2019 find large hydraulic overcapacity and no NSC depletion in lowland species, using percent loss of conductivity as a mortality threshold needs species-specific justification.

### Gaps
- Heat at treeline for conifers (needle temperature exceeding thresholds, radiation-driven): no verified Alpine conifer study. VPD at treeline: only the general Grossiord review.
- Stem hydraulic safety margins (P50, Pmin) for P. cembra, Larix and Picea at treeline: not found in Crossref searches (only roots, Losso 2015).
- Leuschner 2000, Ganthaler papers on P. cembra, Hartmann and Hoch NSC drought papers specific to Alpine: not found; Vittoz 2008 and Gruber 2011 abstracts not read.
- No verified NSC data after drought at Alpine treeline conifers.

## 6. Mechanisms of growth limitation at treeline: sink vs source, meristem temperature, DELLA

### Takeaway
Verified Alpine evidence (all treeline P. cembra work in the Swiss Alps and elsewhere) indicates mobile carbon is not depleted at treeline, which supports sink limitation; thermal limit is a seasonal mean soil/air temperature of roughly 6-7 degC in the Körner and Paulsen framework (value from my background knowledge, UNVERIFIED: abstract not read). Dietrich and Zeidler 2026 propose a hormonal (DELLA) mechanism as a hypothesis only.
