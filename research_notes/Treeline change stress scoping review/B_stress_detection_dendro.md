## Q2. Do Sentinel-2/Landsat anomalies detect drought, heat, frost, snow effects in subalpine/treeline conifers, and have they been compared with tree rings?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence for the Alps is thin and points the same way: closed-canopy stress is detectable at low to mid elevation (Swiss 2018, Trentino 2022), but high-elevation conifer pixels show weaker, null or even positive anomalies (Sturm 2022; Dalponte 2023/2025; Choler 2023 above treeline). No study compares Sentinel-2/Landsat anomalies with tree rings at an Alpine treeline; the only Alpine larch NDVI-plus-growth work I found is a single eddy-covariance site in the Aosta Valley (Oddi 2022) and a satellite-detectable budmoth defoliation at a Swiss site (Peters 2020).

### Inferences
- The S2/Landsat evidence base for treeline stress is: closed-canopy detection works (Löw 2020, prior), high-elevation response is weak or positive in 2018/2022 (rows 1-4), and nothing links S2 to rings at treeline. A null Sentinel-2 anomaly at the forest line in 2018 and 2022 is therefore the evidence-based expectation, not a failure.
- Row 6 (Noyer 2026) is the main caution: it reports a convergence of canopy resilience across altitude in Swiss protective forests, so the null expectation is not safe for declining stands.
- Larch is the species where satellite and ring tools can plausibly meet (row 11: defoliation visible from space and in cell-wall thickness; row 14: strong phenology signal), but a stress signal in larch could be budmoth, not climate.

### Gaps
- No Sentinel-2 or Landsat vs ring-width/density study for *P. cembra*, *L. decidua* or *P. abies* at an Alpine forest line; two web searches (Landsat/NDVI + tree-ring + Alps) returned only Siberian, Mongolian, Mediterranean or lowland Czech work (for example Landsat NDVI vs *Larix sibirica* ring width in Mongolia, [Springer item s10661-021-08996-1 listed in search; not read]).
- No Austrian (Tyrol) subalpine Sentinel-2 drought-anomaly paper found. Dalponte 2025 abstract needs publisher-level reading for elevation breakdown.
- Frost detection by S2 above 1500 m: only the Trentino 2019 beech case; no conifer frost-damage satellite study found.

## Q3. Tree-ring evidence for heat/drought, divergence, 2003/2015/2018/2022, and blue intensity / MXD at the Alpine forest line (P. cembra, L. decidua, P. abies)

### Takeaway
At the Tyrolean/Alpine forest line, summer temperature remains the main control for larch and (less strongly) *P. cembra*; the stone pine is more moisture-sensitive on dry sites (Obojes 2022; Marotta 2026), larch is decoupling from warming in dense subalpine stands (Frank 2026), and the Patscherkofel divergence since the mid-1980s is unresolved (Oberhuber 2008). I found no Alpine treeline ring study that analyses 2018 or 2022 as event years. Blue intensity exists for Alpine larch (Cerrato 2025) and for *P. cembra* (Cerrato 2023), both Rhaetian/Southern Alps, none from Tyrol.

## Q4. Late frost, frost rings, winter desiccation / frost drought and winter-warming effects in *Larix*, *P. cembra*, *Picea*

### Takeaway
Frost rings in Eastern Alpine larch, stone pine and spruce are now documented (Mantovani 2026, Dolomites 1900-2100 m) and show that the frost rings are dated mostly to late May-June cold spells and are rare in old larch and stone pine. Winter desiccation is documented physiologically for *Picea* (not *P. cembra*) and by ring correlation for *P. cembra* at Patscherkofel. Late-frost damage on *P. cembra* is documented once (High Tatras 2024). No recent-winter-warming damage study for larch/stone pine at the Tyrolean forest line was found.

## Q5. Larch budmoth (*Zeiraphera griseana*): dating, recent status in the Eastern Alps, and pest pointers

## Q6. Snow-cover trends at 1900-2300 m in Austria and what is verifiable at forest-line elevation

### Takeaway
Station and model records show strong spring and snow-cover-duration decline below about 2000 m; above about 2000 m the Austrian 1 km model does not show a significant decline, and stations are few. Trends are stronger where the snow/no-snow transition occurs. A 1900-2300 m forest-line stratum therefore sits right at the elevation where the evidence flips.

### Cited Findings
- Matiu, M. et al. (2021) Observed snow depth trends in the European Alps: 1971 to 2019. *Cryosphere* 15(3):1343-1382. [doi](https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-15-1343-2021) [ABSTRACT-READ]. "average trend among all stations for seasonal (November to May) mean snow depth was -8.4% per decade, for seasonal maximum snow depth -5.6% per decade, and for seasonal snow cover duration -5.6% per decade"; "Stronger and more significant trends were observed for periods and elevations where the transition from snow to snow-free occurs"; "regional trends differed substantially at the same elevation". Limit: wiki note says few stations above 2000 m (not in the abstract).
- Olefs, M., Koch, R., Schöner, W., Marke, T. (2020) Changes in snow depth, snow cover duration, and potential snowmaking conditions in Austria, 1961-2020. *Atmosphere* 11(12):1330. [doi](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos11121330) [ABSTRACT-READ] (truncated in CrossRef; prior note: -42 days snow cover duration, significant only below about 2000 m). - Marty's observed snow-trend papers were not identified.
