## Q1. Verification of the 18 core/supporting wiki papers

### Takeaway
Most notes match their PDFs, but nine points need correcting or reframing: (1) Midolo note has the broadleaved-evergreen light trend with the wrong sign; (2) Chelli note lists "EVE expansion" that does not appear in the PDF, and the 03 paper cites Chelli for wildfires although post-fire studies were excluded; (3) the 03 paper cites Noce 2023 for evergreen favouring, but Noce projects a loss of holm-oak suitability in every Apennine section; (4) the 03 paper's "22 % of Italian forest area disturbed" is a Europe-wide number in Soto/Viana-Soto 2025; (5) Gasparini note mixes forest and total wooded land; (6) Herraiz note has a species typo (P. pinea listed as P. nigra) and Herraiz is Andalusia, not Italy; (7) Fady is a species inventory and says nothing on monitoring of laurophylls/sclerophylls; (8) Bayle studied non-forest alpine pixels above 1500 m only; (9) Dyderski, Schuldt, Wessely, Bolyn, Mauri, Calvia contain no evergreen-broadleaf change evidence. Only Conedera, Alessi, Berger, Hiebl 2025 (and, for dieback, Seddaiu 2025 outside this list) are directly about EVE; Conedera and Alessi say climate is secondary.

### Cited Findings

**1. Conedera et al. 2018** [PDF](../../00_literature/conedera_2018_drivers_evergreen.pdf) | [note](../../01_notes/conedera_2018_drivers_evergreen.md)
- Printed header: "Reg Environ Change (2018) 18:425-436, DOI 10.1007/s10113-017-1212-7"; "Received: 3 January 2017 / Accepted: 2 August 2017 / Published online: 15 August 2017" (p.1). Cite as 2018, vol 18, pp 425-436. Issue number not printed.
- Design: 200 plots of 100 m2 on a 100 x 100 m grid, Swiss Lago Maggiore, coldest-month mean temperature gradient -0.4 to 3.0 degC, tobit regression (abstract, p.1). Four target species: native Hedera helix, Ilex aquifolium, exotic Prunus laurocerasus, Trachycarpus fortunei (p.1).
- Abstract: "Propagule pressure resulted to be the main driver of occurrence for three considered species, while meso-climate and stand structure played only a secondary role. In contrast, the presence of native I. aquifolium was mostly correlated with the temperature of the coldest month, though with an overall low impact on cover." Closing: "evergreens invade a largely empty ecological niche, mainly issued by land-use change rather than climate warming" (p.1).
- Climate-history test (p.8): 1975-1985 coldest month 2.25 +/- 1.29 degC at Locarno-Monti; "also registered in earlier decades", 1915-1925 = 2.75 +/- 0.89 degC, 1950-1960 = 2.37 +/- 1.32 degC, "At those times, however, very few gardens with seed-producing evergreens existed". The note reproduces this correctly.
- Land-use context (p.3): of 485 ha terraced vineyards in 1960 only 72 ha (14.8 %) still cultivated in 2010; 59.4 % built over or gardens; 8.9 % became forest. Evergreen spread began 1970s, germination peak mid-1980s (Sittig 1998, quoted p.3).
- Note check: correct. Not verified by me: the note's statement on "averaged cover (herb + shrub + tree layer), arc-sin-sqrt transformed".
- Cite for: evergreen spread into deciduous (chestnut belt) forests since the 1970s in the southern Alps; climate permissive but propagule pressure (distance to gardens) and abandoned coppice dominate; driver set to include in the paper (distance to settlement/gardens, land-use change, stand structure).
- Limits for the Landsat paper: the target taxa are climbers, understory shrubs and a palm (Hedera, Ilex, P. laurocerasus, Trachycarpus), mostly under a closed deciduous canopy (p.3: "closed canopies and hence little light"), and the area is ~100 km2. Detectability from 30 m Landsat of understory evergreens is an open question (inference).

**2. Alessi et al. 2019** [PDF](../../00_literature/alessi_2019_refugia.pdf) | [note](../../01_notes/alessi_2019_refugia.md)
- DOI printed: 10.1111/jvs.12743 ("Please cite this article as doi", p.1). Journal volume/pages not printed in this manuscript (JVS 30:564-574 per TRACEVE/inventory, not verified here).
- 17,087 forest plots; DCA selects 11 native laurophylls; potential range from Beals' index; Abstract (p.2): "Most of them occupied less than half of their estimated potential range. Realized aggregations richest in native laurophylls were localized in the central Apennines. However, the Italian forests showed high potential for native laurophylls with the richest potential aggregations in the whole Apennines and in the southern Alps." Conclusion: "late-Quaternary events could better explain their present-day distribution than ongoing climate change"; "suggests possible future spreading".
- Results (p.10): "9 out of 11 species occupying less than half of their potential range (Table 2). Only Hedera helix and Ruscus aculeatus occupied more than 60%".
- Table 2 realized/potential ratio, % (p.23): Buxus 3.7, Hypericum androsaemum 5.5, Ruscus hypoglossum 6.4, Pyracantha 12, Hypericum hircinum 12.5, Laurus nobilis 18.8, Ilex aquifolium 25.3, Lonicera etrusca 30.4, Daphne laureola 47.3, Ruscus aculeatus 61.3, Hedera helix 71.4. Nine are below 50 %.
- Discussion p.14: "the current climate change plays a secondary role in the spread of native laurophylls when compared to historical processes"; the Insubrian pre-Alps "highly suitable ... independently from the current climate warming". p.15: abandonment of open land and forest ageing "could have led to increased availability of suitable sites"; spreading "is likely to be enhanced in future also by expected milder winter temperatures (Walther, 2001)". Plot-database caveat (p.14): Insubria under-sampled, "might derive from the scarcity of sample plots from that area".
- Note check: correct (both "<50 %" and "9 of 11" are correct, see Q2a). Species classification in the note matches p.10 (ME: Daphne, Ilex, Ruscus hypoglossum, H. androsaemum, Buxus; MA: Hedera, H. hircinum, Pyracantha, Lonicera etrusca, Ruscus aculeatus, Laurus).
- Quercus ilex and other sclerophylls are explicitly excluded from the laurophyll group: "We used a line orthogonal to the first axis to separate laurophylls from sclerophylls" (p.9); Q. ilex appears only in the species list in the appendix table (p.24). The paper therefore does not address holm oak.
- Cite for: Italian laurophylls are in non-equilibrium with climate (potential >> realized), so a spreading trend is plausible; central Apennines are the realized core; Apennines and southern Alps are high-potential zones (useful for hotspot expectations and for the future-habitat question).
- Caution: the laurophyll set is mostly understory shrubs/climbers (Hedera, Ruscus, Daphne, Buxus, Ilex, Hypericum); only Laurus and Ilex reach tree size.

**3. Berger & Walther 2006** [PDF](../../00_literature/berger_2006_distribution_eve.pdf) | [note](../../01_notes/berger_2006_distribution_eve.md)
- Printed: "Bot. Helv. 116 (2006): 65-77", DOI 10.1007/s00035-006-0749-8 (p.1; the note lacks the DOI).
- 22 study sites below 500 m in Insubria (Lago d'Orta to Lago di Garda), species cover and height vs precipitation (about 2000 mm yr-1 west to <1000 mm east) and bedrock (p.3).
- Abstract: "An increasing number of evergreen broad-leaved species has naturalised in deciduous lowland forests near the lakes of southern Switzerland and northern Italy (Insubria), favoured by rising winter temperatures of the last few decades." This is the premise; the study itself tests precipitation and bedrock, not temperature trends (p.1-3).
- Q. ilex (p.9): "A species mainly found in the eastern part of the area is the Mediterranean Quercus ilex"; "this relatively slow growing sclerophyllous species profits on shallow calcareous soils from reduced competition from deciduous species ... This explains the restriction of Q. ilex at its northern fringe of distribution to drier sites with calcareous soils, whereas in the core area of distribution no clear soil preference is apparent". Viburnum tinus shows the same pattern. Q. ilex is classified as indigenous (p.4).
- Note check: correct. Overreach in the 03 paper (line 41): "Sclerophyllous EVE expansion is also a function of competitive dynamics, as they are more drought resistant [berger_2006]"; Berger attributes the calcareous/dry restriction to reduced competition from deciduous and laurophyllous species, not explicitly to drought resistance. Also Berger has no time dimension (single survey, n = 22, EVE-rich stands chosen).
- Cite for: niche partitioning of EVE in Insubria (laurophylls in the humid west on siliceous rock, Q. ilex and V. tinus in the drier east on calcareous soils); precipitation and bedrock as covariates; range-margin species have narrower ecological breadth.

- Note check: findings correct; note typo: species list gives "P. nigra (13.6%), P. nigra (12%)" while the PDF has P. pinea 13.6 % and P. nigra 12 % (p.2). The note title for the paper says "Mediterranean forest species", the PDF title adds "southern Spain".

The phrase "slow changing systems [herraiz]" (line 86) and "(calcareous substrate, core range) [herraiz]" (line 79) do not appear in the PDF; calcareous restriction is from Berger 2006 p.9. NDVI greening there is attributed to stand regrowth/reforestation and CO2, not to EVE displacing deciduous trees.
- Cite for: winter NDVI maximum and summer minimum of evergreen oaks (the phenological basis for separating EVE from deciduous in leaf-off imagery); Q. ilex NDVI trend upward 1994-2021 in southern Spain with regrowth as the proposed cause. Caution: annual/monthly max-NDVI of Landsat 5/7 is exactly the metric that Bayle 2024 shows is sensitive to observation count; Herraiz does not discuss this.

- Cite for: forest area expansion context (p.2: 5.7 Mha in 1954, nearly 9 Mha in 1985; Noce also writes "Italian forests now cover 15 million hectares", which disagrees with Gasparini's 9.09 Mha forest / 11.05 Mha total wooded land, see item 10); projected holm-oak suitability losses as a counter-hypothesis ("future habitats") to be tested; INFC species presence as SDM input. **9. Bayle et al. 2024** [PDF](../../00_literature/bayle_2024_landsat_greening_inflated.pdf) | [note](../../01_notes/bayle_2024_landsat_greening_inflated.md)
- Printed: Ecography 2024: e07394, doi 10.1111/ecog.07394, accepted 22 July 2024 (p.1).
- Abstract: annual maximum NDVI "can be significantly overestimated because the number of available Landsat observations increases over time, and mostly that the magnitude of the overestimation varies along environmental gradients"; "almost 50% of the magnitude of estimated greening can be explained by this bias" in late-snowmelting habitats of the European Alps; recommends reporting number of observations per year.
- Scope limits (p.3-4): analysis restricted to "non-forested land cover classes, tree cover density below 5%, elevation above 1500 m"; summer growing season only (DOY 152-243); MODIS used to simulate Landsat sampling. Effect "was less pronounced in lower-elevation forest ecotone communities" (p.8); false trends up to 30 % [12-50] in the forest ecotone cluster (p.7). Author statement (p.10): "While our study focused on the European Alps and above-forest alpine vegetation ..." and the bias "only becomes a serious problem in certain cases" (p.10-11), with avoidance (restrict to years with enough observations) or correction (phenology-based temporal reconstruction, Berner et al. 2020) strategies.
- Note check: correct. 03 paper line 212 "overestimation of EVE in earlier years with low observation density [bayle]" is an extrapolation: Bayle shows underestimation of annual NDVImax in early years in snow-covered alpine non-forest; it does not address EVE, forest, classifier outputs, or winter.
- Cite for: sampling-density bias of the Landsat archive as a recognised threat to long-term trend analysis, motivating density-controlled design (e.g. sub-sampling experiment) and reporting of observation counts. Do not cite for forest/EVE bias magnitude.

## Q3. Does the draft outline (04_evergreen_distribution_change.md) outrun the wiki evidence?

### Takeaway
Three statements are weakly supported or in tension with the PDFs: "drivers (milder winters etc.)", "dieback of Q. ilex in other areas" (supported but source quality and mapping consequences are not addressed), and the implicit expectation of EVE gain in dry areas and future habitats. The two best-supported statements are spread of EVE into deciduous forests in Insubria and the existence of Q. ilex dieback in central/southern Italy.

### Cited Findings
- **"Spread of evergreens into deciduous forests" (Motivation, Intro):** supported for Insubria by [Berger 2006](../../00_literature/berger_2006_distribution_eve.pdf) p.1 ("An increasing number of evergreen broad-leaved species has naturalised in deciduous lowland forests") and [Conedera 2018](../../00_literature/conedera_2018_drivers_evergreen.pdf) p.1-3; for Italy as a whole only as potential ([Alessi 2019](../../00_literature/alessi_2019_refugia.pdf) p.2, p.10). No wiki PDF quantifies change of EVE cover at national scale; INFC reports +33.6 % "other evergreen broadleaved" area, a residual class that also contains newly formed stands ([Gasparini](../../00_literature/gasparini_2022_nfi_italy.pdf) p.571). The southern-Alps spread documented is mostly of understory/climber/palm species (Conedera p.1; Berger p.4: "Only few indigenous evergreen broad-leaved species are able to grow up to the tree layer, such as Quercus ilex, Ilex aquifolium, Laurus nobilis").
- **"its drivers (milder winters etc.)" (Intro):** only Berger's abstract (p.1) frames winter warming as the cause, as a premise from earlier work; Conedera tests it and finds climate "only a secondary role" and "mainly issued by land-use change rather than climate warming" (p.1), with Ilex aquifolium the only species mostly correlated with the coldest-month temperature "with an overall low impact on cover"; Alessi p.14: "the current climate change plays a secondary role". Expected milder winters are mentioned by Alessi only as a future enhancer (p.15). Kempf 2023 (p.7) reports the winter temperature increase as "particularly" in "the eastern and northern parts of the continent" (GLDAS 1948-2021), and a strong summer increase and soil-moisture decline in the Mediterranean; no wiki PDF documents an Italy winter-warming trend. Midolo 2026 finds temperature indicator values "largely stable" in forests. Suggested rewording: "spread driven by propagule pressure and land-use change, with warming relaxing cold limits".
- **"Dieback and increased mortality of evergreens in Italy and other regions due to a combination of drought and infections" (Intro) and "documented dieback of Q. ilex in other areas" (Motivation):** Q. ilex dieback in Italy is documented in [Seddaiu et al. 2025](../../00_literature/seddaiu_2025_quercus_dieback.pdf) (Forest@ 22:56-65, doi 10.3832/efor4934-022): "first reports of Q. ilex dieback due to climatic events date back to 2012 in Tuscany", more extensive after the 2017 drought, "widespread dieback phenomena" in summer 2024 in Apulia, Basilicata, Sardinia, Sicily; symptoms "partial or total foliar browning, followed by wilting and branch dieback"; "resprouting from dormant buds was observed" (p.1); "often associated with the presence of Phytophthora spp. and other opportunistic pathogens" (p.1). ICP Forests Level I: about 250 Q. ilex trees in 11 plots, defoliation decreased 2001-2016, then increased with peaks in 2017, 2022 and 2025 (Mann-Kendall p<0.05); mortality "sporadic" and "very limited"; death hard to diagnose because of resprouting (p.4). Caveats: "other areas" is in fact inside the study area (central/southern Italy), so dieback overlaps spatially with the EVE trend domain; the paper is a regional overview ("scarcely represented in the current scientific and monitoring programs", p.1). [Satta et al. preprint](../../00_literature/seddaiu_2025_mediterranean_dieback.pdf) (SSRN 5761729, "This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed", p.1): Sentinel-2 dieback detection in Sardinia 2023-24, NGRDI Acc 0.95, kappa 0.71, F1 0.74 (p.2). [Puletti 2019](../../00_literature/puletti_2019_drought_forest_health.pdf) (J. Appl. Remote Sens. 13(2) 020501, Tuscany, 118 samples): beech shows "a strong reduction of VI values in August 2017" and recovers; holm oak "is tolerant to short and occasional extreme events, as confirmed by VI trajectories", and downy oak responds like holm oak (p.7). So the evidence is partly contradictory about holm oak vigour in Tuscany 2017 (Seddaiu: dieback and defoliation peak 2017; Puletti: no VI difference between healthy and unhealthy stands until August 2016 and tolerance).
- **"Spread ... into deciduous forests ... with a combination of drought" implicit (03 paper abstract: "Milder winters and dryer summers favor evergreen broad-leaved species, which might displace deciduous tree species"):** Chelli p.7 reports drought-driven shift from Fagus to Quercus spp. (deciduous oaks included); Bricca 2026 p.2 notes broadleaved evergreen are more drought-tolerant than deciduous (Mediavilla & Escudero 2004) yet "often grow in areas where the severity and frequency of droughts are increasing rapidly"; p.7 predicts a decrease of taxonomic and functional diversity in warm broadleaved-evergreen forests by 2100, "near their physiological tolerance limits", while "at lower elevations, higher temperatures should promote the establishment of tree guilds dominated by locally-adapted drought-resistant broadleaved-evergreen and coniferous tree species" (authors' statements, [Bricca](../../00_literature/bricca_2026_topo_diversity.pdf)). Noce projects holm-oak suitability loss in most sections (see Q1 item 6). Hence the direction of the drought effect on EVE is contested in the collection.
- **Methods "statistical analysis of 40 years of annual LTC and DTS maps":** Bayle (alpine, non-forest) shows archive density bias in max-NDVI trends (p.1, p.7); Hiebl 2025 shows EVE regression overestimates and fails out-of-distribution in conifer stands (p.9); Soto/Viana-Soto omission 22.5 % limits disturbance masking (p.1); no wiki PDF validates Landsat-based EVE cover across 1985-2025.
- **"future prediction of patterns (Hidden Markov?)":** no PDF in the collection deals with HMM or trajectory models; Noce (MaxEnt) and Bricca (regression projections to 2100, +4.4 degC, Fig. 2 p.8) are the only forward-looking Italian studies and both give mixed or negative outlooks for holm oak/warm evergreen forest.
- **"gain and dieback zones":** dieback detection by Landsat: Seddaiu reports resprouting after browning (p.1), Satta uses S2 NGRDI for "sudden canopy browning" (p.2) and found models without climate predictors "consistently overestimated dieback extent". Annual 3-year-window Landsat products may not capture transient browning followed by resprouting (inference).

### Inferences
- The draft's introduction needs a second line of argument for the drivers: land-use/propagule pressure (Conedera, Alessi, Gasparini foreword p.14) rather than only "milder winters".
- Q. ilex dieback may produce false "loss" signals and spatial confounding; a disturbance/dieback mask or separate class for dieback-prone stands (southern Italy, Sardinia, Tuscany) should be planned (inference).
- Holm-oak decline projections (Noce, Bricca) provide a legitimate alternative hypothesis that EVE area might not keep expanding in the south.

### Gaps
- No PDF supplies a verified Italian winter-temperature trend or a mechanistic test of milder winters on Q. ilex/laurophylls; Peñuelas & Boada 2003 and Walther 2002/2005 are missing from 00_literature.
- No PDF provides a Landsat-based EVE time series validation.
