# Untangling land use from climate at Tyrolean treelines

The best-supported conclusion of the Alpine treeline literature is that **the upper forest limit in the Alps is shifting upward at roughly 0.8 to 1.6 m per year in the regions where it has been measured, but that most of this advance is recovery from past land use rather than a response to warming alone**. In the Swiss Alps, only 10% of new forest above 1650 m was a true upward shift and 90% was ingrowth, and only 4% rose above the potential treeline (Gehrig-Fasel et al. 2007). The Innsbruck physiology group describes the Central Austrian treeline as "conservative", with seedling re-establishment reflecting an invasion of potential habitat after reduced grazing (Wieser et al. 2019). For *Pinus cembra* and *Larix decidua*, the dominant mechanism is thermal sink limitation of growth, not carbon shortage (Hoch et al. 2002; Hoch and Körner 2012). Winter processes (frost drought, freeze-thaw embolism, snowmelt timing and snow fungi) are documented mechanisms in spruce, saplings and plantations, but **no verified study shows drought-induced growth decline, mortality or retreat of *P. cembra* or *Larix* at the Tyrolean treeline itself**. Quantitative treeline-position change on the Austrian side of Tyrol has not been published in the peer-reviewed sources found here, and the nearest precedent is a South Tyrolean Landsat study (Menegaldo et al. 2025). The review below is organised by theme, rates the strength of each evidence block, resolves conflicts between sources explicitly, and ends with the knowledge gaps and the implications for a Tyrolean proposal. Almost all numbers come from abstracts, not full texts, and the reference list tags each paper accordingly.

## 1. Scope, evidence conventions and an overview of evidence strength

This review covers the Alpine forest treeline under climate stress, with *P. cembra* and *L. decidua* at the Tyrolean (Eastern Alps) treeline as the focal system. It is Alps-first: non-Alpine work is used only for mechanism and is labelled as such. It draws on four thematic research notes (treeline dynamics, *P. cembra* ecology, stress mechanisms, remote sensing and modelling) and on an earlier project-revision report with its notes, whose corrections are built in. Where the earlier material and the newer notes disagree, the newer notes were taken to prevail and the disagreement is stated.

Bibliographic metadata of every cited paper was checked against Crossref or OpenAlex in the underlying notes. Content claims rest on abstracts unless a paper is tagged F in the reference list (full text read in an earlier note and not re-checked here). Papers tagged M have verified metadata only, so this review uses them for nothing beyond what the title states. Papers tagged P are preprints or conference abstracts. No peer-reviewed study of treeline position change on the Austrian side of Tyrol, no verified drought-mortality record at the Tyrolean treeline, and no verified Alpine treeline UAV-physiology study were found, but the searches behind the notes were limited, so absence of evidence here is not proof of absence.

## 2. Treeline position and dynamics: definitions, rates and the land-use versus climate question

### The measured rate depends on which line is measured

Three boundaries are routinely conflated: the forest line or timberline (closed forest), the tree line (upright trees above a height threshold) and the tree species line (uppermost individual). They respond at different speeds, and a recent perspective argues that many forest edges at high elevation are not climatic treelines at all but limits set by land use or disturbance (Körner and Hoch 2023). The global thermal frame for the climatic limit is a seasonal mean root-zone temperature of **6.7 °C (±0.8 SD) across 46 sites**, 7-8 °C in temperate zones (Körner and Paulsen 2004), which underpins climate-based predictions of the potential treeline (Paulsen and Körner 2014). The practical consequence is that any shift rate is meaningful only with its boundary definition. An Italian aerial-photo preprint reports 1.6 m/yr for the timberline against 1.1 m/yr for the tree species line at the same nine sites, a difference of about 45% (Carrieri et al. 2026, preprint). A deep-learning study of very-high-resolution imagery in the Swiss Alps found that forest boundaries are "highly dependent on the chosen forest definition" (Nguyen et al. 2022). No source found quantifies this definition sensitivity systematically for Tyrol, and no peer-reviewed Austrian source for the numerical height and cover thresholds was located.

### Rates cluster around 1 m per year where measured, and none are Austrian

Rates reported for the Alps are summarised below. They are not like-for-like: they differ in boundary, method and time window.

| Study | Region, period | Method | Result | Evidence level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gehrig-Fasel et al. 2007 | Swiss Alps, 1985-1997 | Land-use statistics | 90% of new forest above 1650 m was ingrowth, 10% true upward shift, 4% above potential treeline | Abstract |
| Frei et al. 2023 | Central Swiss valley, 1972-2012 | Resurvey of all treeline trees >0.5 m | About 10 m per decade (~1 m/yr), maximum 42 m per decade | Abstract |
| Menegaldo et al. 2025 | Schnals valley, South Tyrol, 1985-2023, 16 areas | Landsat plus random forest, orthophoto delineation | Forest cover +44%; advance in 15 of 16 areas, +32 m (Landsat) and +45 m (orthophoto), i.e. about 0.8-1.2 m/yr | Abstract |
| Carrieri et al. 2026 | Nine Italian Alpine sites, 1954-2017 | Aerial images | Timberline 1.6 m/yr, tree species line 1.1 m/yr; ecotone area down about 17% | Preprint, abstract |
| Harsch et al. 2009 | 166 sites worldwide | Meta-analysis | Advance at 52%, no change at 47%, recession at 1% | Abstract |

Two rates sometimes quoted for the Alps are not supported. The figure of 0.4 m/yr over the last century has no Alps-specific source in any verified paper. The 0.354 m/yr northern-hemisphere mean attributed to Lu et al. (2020) was seen only in a search snippet. The western Italian Alps study of Leonelli et al. (2011) is verified bibliographically but its numbers (about 115 m in the 20th century) are not, so this review does not quote them. Vitasse et al. (2021) report that the optimum elevation of woody plants shifted by 32.7 m per decade, but that is the elevation of maximum abundance for 2133 species, not treeline position, and it must not be cited as a treeline rate. For the Austrian side of Tyrol, only physiology, growth, vegetation and modelling studies exist; neither repeat photography, aerial-photo nor Landsat quantification of treeline position was found.

### Land abandonment explains most of the advance, with a documented climate counter-example

The weight of Alpine evidence says land use sets where the forest line sits today. The Swiss land-use statistics place nearly all forest gain within 300 m below the potential treeline and identify land abandonment as the dominant driver (Gehrig-Fasel et al. 2007). A multi-mountain European comparison finds climate signals confounded with pasture abandonment and most variability near the treeline controlled by geomorphology, soil, microclimate and land-use history (Cudlín et al. 2017). A short synthesis argues that interacting ecological, biophysical and human drivers, not climate alone, shape European treelines (Kulakowski et al. 2016). In Tyrol and South Tyrol, grassland studies identify land-use change as the main driver of vegetation change in the Passeier valley (Tasser and Tappeiner 2002), and the same group documents natural reforestation after abandonment in the Eastern Central Alps (Tasser et al. 2007; abstract not read, title-level only). The Eastern Alpine iLand simulation of Marzini et al. (2025) reaches the same view by modelling: the current ecotone is mainly land-use constrained and forest expands rapidly after abandonment regardless of warming, while climate mainly affects composition at higher elevations. For *P. cembra* itself, grazing nearly suppressed stone-pine establishment in the western Italian Alps between 1850 and 1930 and regeneration was massive after 1960 (Motta et al. 2006), and in the dry Central Swiss Alps recruitment began about 1850 at an undisturbed site and about 60 years later at a grazed one (Vittoz et al. 2008). The Innsbruck group's review states that the Central Austrian treeline "behaves in a conservative way" and that observed re-establishment is an invasion of potential habitat after reduced grazing (Wieser et al. 2019).

The counter-position is not empty. In the southern French Alps, a marked acceleration of larch establishment since the early 2000s is attributed primarily to climate, with 19th-century pastoral abandonment only creating the predisposing conditions (Nicoud et al. 2025). The dispute is better read as a difference of time window and ecological setting than as a true contradiction: abandonment created the opportunity, and recent warming may speed colonisation where that opportunity exists. No study separates the two contributions in Tyrol. The Pyrenean analogue, titled as land-use legacies rather than climate driving the recent shift (Ameztegui et al. 2016), supports the land-use reading but is non-Alpine and was verified by metadata only.

An evidence caveat applies to the "conservative treeline" position: it rests on a small number of authors working largely on one mountain (Patscherkofel) and nearby sites. Independent replication at other Tyrolean treelines was not found.

### Holocene records agree on a higher past limit but not on how much of today's gap is anthropogenic

Palaeo-records show the Alpine timberline was higher in the early and middle Holocene. In the Swiss Central Alps, timberline and treeline maxima lay at about 2420 and 2530 m, **120-180 m above today's**, in *P. cembra*-*Larix* forests, and the treeline ecocline was only 100-150 m wide (Tinner and Theurillat 2003). They read today's treeless alpine zone as largely natural. A 12,000-year record from a Swiss lake at 2343 m plus a dynamic simulation reads the same period differently: fire is a natural disturbance, but increased land use and anthropogenic fire during the last 4000 years changed species composition, with *P. cembra* benefiting from July temperatures above the modern mean unless fire was severe, and heavy burning replacing *Larix*-*P. cembra* forest by open *Juniperus nana* land (Colombaroli et al. 2010). These views are compatible on the early-Holocene height of the limit and diverge on attribution. Tinner and Theurillat speak to the width and stability of the ecocline, Colombaroli et al. to composition under fire and grazing. Neither is Tyrolean and neither can settle how much of the present Tyrolean forest-line depression is anthropogenic. The one Tyrolean subfossil-wood study of this kind (Kauner valley; Nicolussi et al. 2005) was verified by metadata only, so its elevations are not available here. This review therefore treats "the present forest line lies below the climatic potential in much of the Alps" as supported for the Swiss Central Alps, probable for Tyrol, and the anthropogenic share as unresolved. The title of Tinner and Theurillat's paper is listed as "11,500 years" in OpenAlex and "11,700" in Crossref; check the publisher page.

### Climate has changed strongly, but treeline-elevation snow and drought trends are thin

The Greater Alpine Region warmed by about 1.2 °C over the 20th century, with a second step of 1.3 °C per 25 years from the 1970s (Auer et al. 2007), and warming runs at about 0.36 °C per decade since 1970 (Vitasse et al. 2021). Snow has declined too, but the picture is elevation- and season-dependent. Matiu et al. (2021) report decreasing snow depth at most stations, with seasonal mean depth falling 8.4% per decade and seasonal snow-cover duration 5.6% per decade, and large spring losses at 1000-3000 m. The notes disagree on the station count (more than 800 in one, more than 2000 in the other) and on the share of declining stations (87% in one), so counts should be copied from the paper itself. Snow water equivalent declines at most Alpine sites, more strongly in spring, and even the highest sites show a spring decline (Marty et al. 2017). Austrian modelling shows a mean 0.15 m loss of seasonal snow depth and 42 fewer days of snow cover since 1961/62, with relative reductions smaller at high elevation (Olefs et al. 2020), and Swiss records attribute shorter snow cover more to earlier melt than later onset (Klein et al. 2016, title-level only). A treeline at 2000-2300 m therefore sits where spring snow loss operates, but **no verified source gives snow-cover duration at Tyrolean treeline elevations**, and warming-driven drought at treeline elevation is a projection (Gobiet et al. 2014), not an observed trend. Büntgen et al. (2011) place the recent warming as unprecedented in 2500 years of Central European tree-ring data, which supports the framing but does not speak to treeline response.

### Lag is asserted more than measured

The only Tyrol-specific lag statement is a conference abstract: uppermost *P. cembra* individuals in Eastern Tyrol (about 2500 m) lag substantially behind a roughly 2 K isotherm shift, with fast-radiating sites under nutcracker control (Körner 2024, conference abstract). The abstract is citable only as such. A montane study found no altitudinal shift of *P. sylvestris* and *P. cembra* after 0.8 °C summer warming but large seedling banks, suggesting stabilisation by species interactions (Hättenschwiler and Körner 1995). A spatially explicit Austrian simulation, for *Pinus mugo* rather than *P. cembra* or *Larix*, found that dispersal and invasibility limit expansion at least as much as 0-2 °C warming (Dullinger et al. 2004). A sound reading is that the gap between the forest line and the potential line has two components, historic land-use depression and demographic lag from generation time and dispersal, and no study separates them. No peer-reviewed lag estimate for Alpine treelines was found.

### Evidence for stagnation or retreat is mechanistic, not demographic

Direct documentation of retreat is rare. Harsch et al. (2009) found recession at 1% of 166 sites, and the nine Italian sites of Carrieri et al. (2026, preprint) all advanced. Stagnation is explained by wind and Foehn on south and southeast slopes in South Tyrol (Menegaldo et al. 2025), by avalanche disturbance (Bebi et al. 2009, title-level only), by dispersal limitation, and by the winter processes reviewed in section 5. The apparent contradiction between physiological studies describing mortality mechanisms and observational studies showing advance is not necessarily real, since mortality is local and timing-dependent, but no study scales physiological stress to boundary shifts. No study linking treeline stagnation to the 2003, 2015, 2018 or 2022 droughts in the Eastern Alps was found, and no verified Tyrolean quantification of ungulate browsing at the forest line exists.

## 3. *Pinus cembra* ecology and regeneration: dispersal, microsite and the early-life bottleneck

### Growth is sink-limited and carbon is not the constraint

*P. cembra* is the long-lived, bird-dispersed pine of the continental inner Alps that forms the upper forest limit with *L. decidua*. Along a 110 m transect at the Swiss treeline, non-structural carbohydrates and lipids in all organs increased with elevation and were never depleted, with lipids 50-75% of mobile carbon in wood, which led the authors to conclude growth is sink-limited (Hoch et al. 2002). A comparison across 13 high-elevation regions found no depletion either (Hoch and Körner 2012). In seedlings, deciduous *Larix* slightly benefited from variable low temperatures where evergreen *P. mugo* did not (Hoch and Körner 2009, with *P. mugo* as the pine, so not *P. cembra*), and basal area increment decreased with elevation in both *Larix* and *P. cembra* while NSC increased, with deciduous species holding higher NSC than evergreens (Fajardo et al. 2013). A Tyrolean soil-warming experiment at the treeline raised whole-tree sap flow of *P. cembra* by 11% and 19% in the second and third year, probably through root production and conductivity, while leaf-level net CO2 uptake hardly changed (Wieser et al. 2015; six treated and six control trees). Nitrogen addition and understory removal, but not soil warming, increased radial growth at a Sellrain-valley treeline (Gruber et al. 2018; six trees per treatment, three seasons), pointing to nutrient and root competition. A viewpoint proposes hormonal (DELLA) regulation as an alternative to carbon limitation but presents no conifer data (Dietrich and Zeidler 2026), and at Stillberg 9 years of CO2 enrichment raised growth in *Larix* only, not in the evergreen pine (Dawes et al. 2011). Taken together, the earlier claim that there is "no direct evidence on carbon versus growth limitation in conifers" is false for carbon and true only for the hormonal hypothesis. What is untested is whether stress-year carbon dynamics (after drought or frost) differ from these temperature-limited baselines.

### Seed dispersal by nutcrackers and microsite conditions both constrain recruitment, and the studies disagree

The spotted nutcracker (*Nucifraga caryocatactes*) is the key disperser. Seed transplants at and beyond upper and lower range limits in the Swiss Alps found significant effects of biotic interactions and nutcracker seed deposition and no significant abiotic effect, so dispersal rather than temperature or soil moisture limited recruitment at the upper range (Neuschulz et al. 2018). Nutcrackers cached at sites with low canopy openness and low soil moisture, whereas seedling establishment was positively related to canopy openness and soil moisture, so caching and establishment sites have opposed conditions (Neuschulz et al. 2014). Short-distance caches carried higher shrub cover and higher soil moisture and were higher in elevation, so upward shifts are enabled primarily by short- rather than long-distance dispersal (Trabold et al. 2026, abstract read, author list possibly incomplete). Species distribution models projecting only minor upward shifts for *P. cembra* alongside a northward shift of its disperser (Engelhardt et al. 2019; full title not retrieved in the source note) are consistent with this pattern.

A transect study of 10 Swiss tree species reports the opposite emphasis: seedlings and saplings stood on average **73 m above adult trees**, leading the authors to conclude that neither dispersal nor establishment is a serious limitation at upper limits under current conditions (Vitasse et al. 2012). The two Swiss studies are reconcilable. Seedlings can exist above adults while their density is dispersal-driven, and transplant experiments measure different quantities from transect snapshots. The discrepancy remains unresolved for Tyrol because neither study sampled there.

Early establishment is a bottleneck with species-specific moisture-temperature interactions. A French Alps experiment at 2100 m with warming and watering on five species including *L. decidua*, *P. cembra* and *P. uncinata* found establishment limited by either temperature or moisture, often one depending on the other, and responses highly species-specific (Loranger et al. 2016; first season only), while the role of grassland ranged from competition to facilitation (Loranger et al. 2017, abstract truncated). A global synthesis of 52 studies from 26 countries found that microsite effects reverse between cold-wet and cold-dry climates (Johnson and Yeakley 2019). Microsites chosen by *P. cembra*, *L. decidua* and *P. uncinata* in the French Alps were similar, usually sheltered, and mostly krummholz or bent individuals (Ramírez et al. 2024). In Tyrol, young trees in the Schmirn valley (1900-2100 m) showed an elevation effect on growth only above 0.5 m height, and canopy closure above 3 m created forest microclimate (Li and Yang 2004; plantation-aged trees, not adults). Survival is lower for *P. cembra* than *Larix*: in the Stillberg planting of 92,000 seedlings, pines survived at a much lower rate than larch because of indirect pathogen interactions with snow fungi (Lechler et al. 2024). Planted-stand results may not transfer to natural stands.

Several basic items are absent from the verified literature: masting and seed production of *P. cembra*, quantified browsing effects on regeneration in Tyrol, and an Alpine tree-island or krummholz study. Seed predation by red squirrels and other consumers (Zong et al. 2010) is verified by metadata only. The North American whitebark pine dispersal mutualism (Tomback 1982), including 56% first-year seedling survival from caches, is a conceptual reference and cannot be transferred quantitatively.

## 4. Dendroecology: Patscherkofel winter signals, age effects and the larch budmoth

### Growth responds to previous-autumn and winter conditions at the Tyrolean treeline

At Patscherkofel (Central Tyrol, plots up to 2246 m), *P. cembra* ring width correlated positively with July temperature at the timberline, with a mild previous autumn and with January-March precipitation. At the treeline previous-autumn temperature and March precipitation controlled growth and July temperature was not significant, and south- and west-facing stands were more sensitive in the 1990s, which the author interpreted as support for a winter desiccation hypothesis (Oberhuber 2004). The earlier project-revision work adds that the same paper tentatively links post-1980s divergence to "extreme events or increasing drought stress", the nearest verified suggestion of drought at a Tyrolean treeline, and it is a tentative statement in one paper. A study of Patscherkofel tree-ring-climate relationships since the mid-1980s (Oberhuber et al. 2008) and Pfeifer et al.'s analysis of distinct growth reductions over 200 years (Pfeifer et al. 2005) are verified by metadata only, so their findings cannot be used here.

Across 30 treeline *P. cembra* chronologies in the French and Italian Alps, there was no spatial pattern in growth statistics, local factors (soil moisture, snowmelt, radiation, stand history and land use) dominated, and winter conditions and spring-summer temperature acted mainly through growing-season length (Carrer et al. 2007). This is consistent with Oberhuber (2004) once one accepts that a mix of winter-limited and summer-limited response types exists at different sites; the same fact explains the lack of regional pattern.

### Age, non-stationarity and insects complicate any climate signal

Climate-growth relations change with tree age. The variance explained by climate and the share of trees with a significant response increased with age in both species, response patterns changed with age in *Larix* (increasing for trees under 200 years) but tree-ring statistics were age-stable in *P. cembra*, and non-stratified sampling biases chronologies (Carrer and Urbinati 2004). Across 17 treeline *Larix* sites, chronologies were synchronous but the most sensitive climate variables showed transient responses, a possible deviation from the uniformitarian principle (Carrer and Urbinati 2006). These two findings mean that juvenile and adult trees cannot be pooled and that non-stationarity must be tested rather than assumed away.

*Larix* chronologies carry a non-climatic signal that *P. cembra* lacks. The larch budmoth (*Zeiraphera diniana*) produced regular outbreaks every 9.3 years over 1173 years, persisting through the Medieval warming and Little Ice Age and absent after 1981 (Esper et al. 2007). Alpine-wide, outbreaks recur every 8-9 years in 5000+ series from 70 larch and 73 spruce sites and have ceased in recent decades (Büntgen et al. 2009), and warming shifted outbreak epicentres in elevation toward the larch distribution limit (Johnson et al. 2010). Outbreak waves travel northeast-east at about 210 km per year (Bjørnstad et al. 2002). The inference is that any 20th-century stationarity test on *Larix* must correct for budmoth signals, and that *P. cembra* can serve as a non-host reference chronology (a method not tested in the notes). No Tyrol-specific post-2000 outbreak documentation was found. Anomalous recent *Larix* decoupling from temperature has been reported (Frank et al. 2026), but the notes read only metadata, so the finding is not used here.

### Wood anatomy and phenology refine what ring width can say

Maximum latewood radial cell-wall thickness in *P. cembra* at a high Engadin stand explained **62% (interannual) and more than 80% (decadal) of air-temperature variability** over 1920-2017, outperforming earlier *P. cembra* ring-width work (Lopez-Saez et al. 2023, single stand), and a follow-up reconstructed glacier mass balance from multiproxy records (Lopez-Saez et al. 2024). For larch, ring formation in the Lötschental ran mid-May to end of October, season length fell from 137 to 101 days with elevation, and onset moved 3-4 days per 100 m (Moser et al. 2010, one year). Temperature alone explained onset of tracheid production in *Larix* and spruce across a 900 m gradient while water potential was needed to explain cessation and total tracheid number (Cabon et al. 2020). The Tyrolean intra-annual xylogenesis studies of the Patscherkofel group (Gruber et al. 2009, wood formation and radial increment) are metadata-only here. A Tyrolean study of tree-ring growth increase possibly CO2-related (Nicolussi et al. 1995, title-level only) is contradicted in spirit by the sink-limitation evidence in section 3, but its content was not read, so no contradiction is asserted.

Frost-ring and pointer-year analyses for *P. cembra* and *Larix* at the treeline (2003, 2015, 2018, 2022) and juvenile-specific treeline sensitivity were not found. This is the main dendroecological gap relevant to stress.

## 5. Snow, frost and drought physiology: strong winter mechanisms, no adult-tree test, and thin summer drought evidence

### Winter desiccation and freeze-thaw embolism are real but best documented in spruce

At the Tyrolean timberline, embolism occurred only at the timberline and only in Norway spruce (up to 49.2% loss of conductivity, water potentials to -3.5 MPa), not in stone pine, and embolism decreased from late winter (Mayr et al. 2003). Mayr et al. (2002) recorded up to 100% loss at 2020 m with water potentials to -4.0 MPa, and later work recorded up to 86.2% loss in spruce with refilling starting in February while soil was frozen, supported by branch water uptake (Mayr et al. 2014). These figures differ, which reflects different winters and trees and is not a contradiction. Embolism formation in spruce twigs increased with the number of freeze-thaw cycles after dehydration to -2.8 MPa (Mayr et al. 2007). Exposed crown parts showed high conductivity loss despite moderate water potential, and smaller trees were more affected (Mayr and Charra-Vaskou 2007).

For *P. cembra*, the evidence is of avoidance, and the 2003 comparison is a single timberline contrast of two winters (the authors hypothesise avoidance). A Dolomites study found low minimum cuticular conductance in *P. cembra* that did not change with altitude (Anfodillo et al. 2002), and a Patscherkofel study found winter gas exchange nearly completely suppressed with total winter carbon loss equal to one or two warm spring or summer days (Wieser 1997). A review of frost resistance argues that premature deacclimation in spring, rather than absolute winter minima, may be critical for *P. cembra* under projected change (Neuner 2014). Buchner and Neuner (2011) measured winter frost resistance of *P. cembra* in situ at the timberline (metadata only). The closest verified link between frost and frost-ring-like damage is that a frost event early in ring formation caused a non-lignified "blue" layer without cambial damage, while chronic 3 K cooling did not change lignification (Körner et al. 2023; species not stated in the abstract excerpt). Soil frost and root water uptake in Alpine treeline conifers are the least-documented part: no verified Alpine study exists, and the New Hampshire snow-manipulation work (Groffman et al. 2001) is mechanistic context only (title-level).

### Snow is protective in one setting and hazardous in another

The single Alpine snow-removal experiment used saplings of *Acer*, *Juniperus*, *Larix*, *Picea* and *Sorbus* at 1700 m. Snow-free saplings had higher spring loss of conductivity and more living-cell mortality, more winter dehydration and lower growth, and summer mortality correlated with spring hydraulic damage (Charra-Vaskou et al. 2026). *P. cembra* was not tested, and 1700 m is at or below the Tyrolean timberline, so the experiment sits below the ecotone. The same paper provides the only (indirect) support for sequential winter-to-summer damage.

In the opposite direction, long snow cover is itself harmful. In the 30-year Stillberg experiment, **snowmelt date was always the most important factor for mortality** in *Larix*, *P. mugo* ssp. *uncinata* and *P. cembra*, while elevation was most important for growth (Barbeito et al. 2012). Snow-fungus-induced mortality of *P. cembra* plantations is documented at the treeline (Barbeito et al. 2013, title-level only), and pines survived much worse than larch at Stillberg (Lechler et al. 2024). A third mechanism applies where melt is early. Seedlings of five species in the French Alps showed lower Fv/Fm in the first weeks after emergence when emerging early (about two weeks earlier), shading reduced stress and night warming did not, i.e. radiation and frost after emergence, not snow loss itself (Bader et al. 2018; below the regional treeline). These mechanisms act at different microsites, so the verdict "less snow is bad" or "less snow is good" is wrong in both forms. For adult trees above the snowpack, snow changes matter mainly via soil frost, spring phenology and drought timing, and no adult-tree evidence was retrieved. A pitfall in earlier project text should be noted here: the claim that "seasonal snow cover explains treeline better than temperature" belongs to a study of 11,804 eastern Himalayan locations (Huang et al. 2023) and is not an Alpine finding, and a facilitation experiment cited for seedling survival is Tibetan (Chen et al. 2024).

### Frost-risk trends with elevation disagree between studies

For eight deciduous species at their upper limits in the Swiss Alps, freeze exposure did not increase with elevation and safety margins against spring freeze stayed constant at 5-8.5 K because flushing is later at altitude (Lenz et al. 2013). In contrast, an analysis of 13 species at 264 Swiss sites over 60+ years found safety margins sufficient but tending to decrease with elevation, with leaf unfolding advancing most at higher elevations (Bigler and Bugmann 2018). The species sets, methods and time frames differ and the studies are on deciduous broadleaves; neither covers *P. cembra*, *Larix* or spruce. The disagreement is therefore unresolved for the focal conifers. No verified evidence shows that late frost reduces *Larix* growth at the Alpine treeline.

### Summer drought limits larch at lower elevation, not demonstrably at the treeline

Water limitation is documented below the treeline. In a dry inner-Alpine South Tyrolean valley, *Larix* sap flow and growth were reduced in dry periods up to 1715 m, with maximum annual growth at 2000 m and a temperature response only above 2000 m (Obojes et al. 2018; the tested range reached 2250 m but showed no drought effect at the top). A later multi-species gradient study reports that at high elevation *P. cembra*'s temperature response was smaller than expected while *Larix* responded positively and grew faster in recent decades (Obojes et al. 2024; the companion paper, Obojes et al. 2022, is titled as *P. cembra* benefiting less from warming than larch at a dry forest line, metadata only). In the Swiss Valais, larch did not cope with dry conditions after irrigation ceased (Eilmann and Rigling 2012), and a 104-site tree-ring study to 2200 m found larch and spruce growth strongly and enduringly reduced by spring droughts, whereas oak and beech recovered from late frost (Vitasse et al. 2019). *P. cembra* was not part of that analysis. The only Alpine drought-signal evidence for *P. cembra* near the treeline is a drought signal in the lowest stands of the dry Central Swiss Alps ecotone (Vittoz et al. 2008). At the treeline, three years of rain exclusion did not reduce sap flow in *P. abies* and *L. decidua* saplings (Wieser et al. 2019 on drought), and roofing that excluded about 33% of precipitation caused no soil-water shortage (Gruber et al. 2018). Lévesque et al. (2013) show high drought vulnerability of spruce and larch, but at low-elevation dry sites and without *P. cembra*, so they do not bear on the treeline.

The evidence on stress-year legacy is similarly indirect. Global tree-ring data show growth reductions for 3-4 years after drought (Anderegg et al. 2015), and lowland experiments show hydraulic legacy effects in spruce (Knüver et al. 2022), but neither concerns Alpine treelines. Lowland work also shows large hydraulic overcapacity (Dietrich et al. 2018), so percent loss of conductivity as a mortality threshold needs species-specific justification. Whole-tree water use of *P. cembra* at the treeline averaged 1.9 mm per day for three trees, with irradiance having a larger effect on sap flow than vapour-pressure deficit (Wieser and Leo 2012). Stem hydraulic safety margins (P50, minimum water potential) for *P. cembra*, *Larix* and spruce at the treeline were not found. The sensible inference is that drought is a lower-elevation constraint and winter and cold stress dominate at the treeline, which is untested as a statement and remains a hypothesis.

## 6. Remote sensing and modelling: forest-line precedents exist, treeline physiology and validated projections do not

### Landsat can detect forest-line shifts of tens of metres, but not the individual-tree line

The notes contain a conflict. The earlier methods note held that 30 m Landsat cannot detect treeline displacement, and gave 8-20 m over 40 years at 0.2-0.5 m/yr as the reason. A later South Tyrolean study found a mean forest-boundary advance of **+32 m by Landsat and +45 m by orthophoto over 1985-2023 in 15 of 16 areas**, with manual and random-forest lines agreeing within one Landsat pixel (30 m) in most cases (Menegaldo et al. 2025). The two statements are reconcilable and both partly right. The earlier claim is correct for rates at or below about 0.5 m/yr and for an individual-tree line, where cumulative displacement is below one pixel. It is too strong as a blanket statement for a forest-cover boundary advancing near 1 m/yr, where the cumulative shift exceeds one pixel and a supervised classifier can see it. Menegaldo et al. also show a negative bias: Landsat gave about 13 m less advance than orthophotos (arithmetic on the two means), so orthophotos are the better reference. Caveats are a single valley, 16 areas, and abstract-level evidence. A related Landsat paper detects greenness and wetness trend hotspots along a Copernicus-derived forest line and does not measure displacement; trend against elevation was not significant (Baglioni et al. 2025, full text read in an earlier note). The usable conclusion is that Landsat supports forest-line densification and trend detection, orthophotos support line displacement, and the choice must follow the boundary definition.

Greenness is not advance. Landsat EVI trends at 74 Yukon treeline ecotones showed 27.5% positive pixels, with greening highest in non-treed pixels (40.8%) and shrubs (30.5%) and lowest in forest (13.3-18.9%) (Bolton et al. 2018; not Alpine). A meta-analysis found only about 10% of treeline studies use remote sensing, long-term studies rely on aerial photographs, recent ones on pixel greenness, and only 19% use multi-scale, multi-sensor designs (Garbarino et al. 2023). Historical aerial images with convolutional networks can map 30-year woody change at fine resolution but classify sparse tree groups, the treeline class, weakly (Wang et al. 2022; the study area is not stated in the abstract and the first-author surname is as recorded in the notes). Austrian airborne laser scanning of the treeline specifically was not found.

### Individual trees and species at treeline are detectable only with centimetre imagery, and only partly

Drone imagery at 5 cm detected 67% of trees overall and only **52% of trees up to 130 cm** at Italian Alpine treelines (F1 0.76, falling to 0.68 under spatially independent validation; Carrieri et al. 2025, full text read in an earlier note). Species classification with 0.31-1.24 m WorldView-3 reached 44-47% for multi-class problems in the Colorado Rockies, not the Alps (Sindewald et al. 2025), and a foundation-model paper shows no treeline data and no advantage of off-the-shelf SAM over a trained Mask R-CNN (Teng et al. 2025). Closed-forest benchmarks are better but not transferable: Faster R-CNN reached mean F1 0.76 (0.59 for Scots pine, 0.92 in the multi-species model) in Swiss forests (Beloiu et al. 2023), and Sentinel-2 time cubes reached F1 0.89-0.93 for 13 woody species in the Tatras against 0.87-0.95 for 2 m hyperspectral and 3 m Planet (Kluczek et al. 2023). Alpine treeline species mapping and saplings below 50 cm are not demonstrated anywhere in the verified literature.

### Satellite drought-stress mapping is weakly resolved at the treeline

The 2018 drought was unprecedented in Central Europe. Sentinel-2 showed early wilting across 21,500 ± 2,800 km² mainly in Germany and Czechia with small and medium trees, steep slopes and shallow soils at higher risk (Brun et al. 2020a). MODIS NDVI showed a stronger 2018 than 2003 impact, with forests less affected than pasture and arable land (Buras et al. 2020), and a ground-based Austria-Germany-Switzerland synthesis recorded mean April-October temperatures more than 3.3 °C above the long-term mean, foliar water potentials beyond hydraulic-failure thresholds, mortality and strong 2019 legacy effects (Schuldt et al. 2020). Disturbance reached the edge of its past range across Europe (Senf and Seidl 2021). These studies resolve broadleaf and lowland conifer stands, not treeline conifers with crowns under 5 m, and the response of high-elevation stands is not resolved. A Sentinel-2 phenology model for all Austrian forest pixels exists (Löw and Koukal 2020), without a treeline-specific accuracy statement. No verified Sentinel-2 anomaly study for Tyrolean subalpine forests exists. Snow masking is the mountain-specific risk: MODIS snow trends have larger uncertainty in complex terrain, and webcam snow mapping has a georectification error of 14.1 m for standard lenses in the Swiss Alps (Portenier et al. 2020).

For UAV physiology, the verified work is from orchards and plantations. Airborne SIF and thermal indices vary with within-crown heterogeneity, and sunlit-crown and whole-crown retrievals differ (Camino et al. 2018, almond orchard). SIF seasonality in a high-elevation evergreen forest in Colorado is shaped by sustained non-photochemical quenching, so it is not simply stress (Raczka et al. 2019), and the photochemical reflectance index in evergreen pines is driven by three processes on different timescales, mainly the seasonal carotenoid-to-chlorophyll ratio (Wong and Gamon 2014). Instrument-level issues of UAV-SIF are reviewed in Bendig et al. (2020) and Vargas et al. (2020). No UAV SIF or thermal study on Alpine conifers, steep terrain or small treeline trees exists, so applying these methods at the treeline is extrapolation.

### Distribution and process models project potential, not realised, treelines

Correlative models transfer poorly: for 54 plant species in Switzerland and Austria, transferability failed for 68% (CH) and 55% (AT) of species with GLMs (Randin et al. 2006; plants, not trees). A study of 34 European tree species found that intermediate complexity performs best and that multicollinearity increases projected range loss (Brun et al. 2020b), and spatial or block cross-validation avoids underestimating error (Roberts et al. 2017). Process models stress dispersal and recruitment limitation (Dullinger et al. 2004) and topographic buffering: Albrich et al. (2020) simulate an Eastern Alpine landscape in which warming above about 2 °C triggers a shift to smaller, broadleaf-dominated forest, with topography delaying the transition. That study concerns montane to subalpine composition, not treeline position. Fine-scale topography creates thermal mosaics (Scherrer and Körner 2011), and in-situ soil-temperature data improved distribution-model R² by 16% in Scandinavian mountains (Lembrechts et al. 2019; alpine plants, not conifers). Observation-based snow and temperature products exist for Austria: SPARTACUS temperature grids with leave-one-out MAE of about 1 °C, larger for minima and winter (Hiebl and Frei 2016), and SNOWGRID-CL at 1 km (Olefs et al. 2020). EURO-CORDEX at 12 km projects 25% (RCP4.5) and 45% (RCP8.5) less September-May snowfall by end-century but has positive snowfall biases at high elevations before bias correction (Frei et al. 2018). The Marzini et al. (2025) study is the only Eastern Alpine treeline-specific projection; no model has been validated against an observed Alpine treeline shift.

## 8. Implications for the proposal

These points follow from the evidence above and should be read as design implications, not recommendations on funding or scope. First, the premise that drought and snow loss are compounding stressors at the Tyrolean treeline is a hypothesis, not a finding, and the framing "test whether drought has become co-limiting" is the supportable one. Second, land-use history must enter every analysis as a covariate, since the weight of Alpine evidence makes it the leading explanation for current forest-line position. Third, treeline position must be defined explicitly (forest line, tree line or tree species line), because rates differ by up to 45% between boundaries. Fourth, remote-sensing ambitions should match pixel sizes: Landsat for forest-line densification and trend, orthophotos for displacement, drones at centimetre resolution for crowns, and no SIF-based stress claim without a ground-truthed pilot. Fifth, a recruitment aim needs recruitment measurement, and the nutcracker-versus-microsite conflict means seed-source distance and cache-like sheltered microsites must be recorded. Sixth, the strongest design choices are those that work in a normal year: adult ring series for retrospective drought years, loggers for snow and soil frost, and an explicit physiological ground truth. Seventh, the treeline carbon-limitation literature is already decisive for *P. cembra*, so carbon should be treated as a measurable response to stress years, not as the default mechanism.

## Conclusion

Three things change relative to a drought-and-snow framing. The Tyrolean treeline literature reads as a story of recovery from land use and thermal sink limitation, with winter processes as the best-evidenced stressors and summer drought as a lower-elevation phenomenon, and every source that argues the opposite is either a preprint, a different region or a different species. The apparent conflicts in the literature are mostly differences of definition and scale: Landsat can see forest-line shifts of tens of metres but not tree lines, Tinner and Theurillat and Colombaroli et al. agree on the early-Holocene height of the limit and differ on attribution, Vitasse et al. (2012) and Neuschulz et al. (2018) measure different aspects of recruitment, and the Lenz and Bigler-Bugmann frost-margin results concern different species sets. And the decisive evidence is missing exactly where a Tyrolean study would be positioned: adult *P. cembra* and *Larix* under stress years at the ecotone, the recruitment and browsing data, and any quantified Austrian forest-line shift.
