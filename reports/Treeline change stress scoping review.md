## 1. Protocol

### 1.1 Review questions
1. What is the evidence on position change of the Alpine forest line / treeline (rates, boundary definitions, structure) and on its drivers (climate, land-use legacy, browsing, disturbance), with Austrian Tyrol as the focus?
2. What is the evidence on stress at the forest line (heat and drought, frost and winter desiccation, snow change, pests) and on its detection with Sentinel-2 / Landsat and tree rings?
3. Which remote-sensing and modelling methods have been used for forest-line change (Landsat time series, 3D / ALS / stereo, deep-learning delineation) and for driver attribution with uncertainty, and what are their documented detection limits?
4. Do the evidence and the concepts support the gap statements, research questions and design choices of the project proposal?

### 1.2 PCC framework
| Element | Definition used |
|---|---|
| Population | The Alpine forest-line / treeline ecotone and its tree species, especially *Pinus cembra*, *Larix decidua*, *Picea abies* (and *Pinus mugo* / *Alnus viridis* as ecotone shrubs where a source treats them) |
| Concept | Position and structure change (rates, ingrowth vs advance), drivers (climate, land-use legacy, browsing, avalanche / geomorphic disturbance), stress (heat and drought, frost and winter, snow change, pests), detection and attribution methods and their uncertainty |
| Context | Eastern and Central Alps with Tyrol focus; South Tyrol, Dolomites, Trentino, Swiss and French Alps as comparators; other mountains (Pyrenees, Norway, Scotland, Tianshan, Rockies, Taiwan, Nepal) only for mechanism or method |

- Chart studies by theme, design, region, period, target, data or method and finding.
- Define and source the key concepts; flag terms without a verified source (for example "minimum detectable shift").
- Identify what is established, contested and missing per Introduction storyline.
- Test each research gap, research question and design choice of the proposal and give a verdict with suggested wording.
- Give the author a reading starting point and a search plan that includes the Scopus / Web of Science search no note performed.

### 1.6 Selection and charting process
- One reviewer (AI-assisted). Researchers (model agents) searched, read what was reachable and tagged the verification level; the synthesis step reused those tags without upgrading them.
- Selection was iterative and judgement-based; no dual screening, no kappa. Items judged weak, snippet-level or off-topic were dropped or labelled cite-only (section 6.3).
- Charting: one row per study or closely linked study group with label, reference and DOI, region and period, target and method, finding (verbatim only where a note has the quote), design limit, tag and wiki link (section 5). Each row has one primary theme: **A** = position change and drivers; **B** = stress and its detection; **C** = methods and attribution.
- No formal risk-of-bias appraisal. Tags describe verification depth, not study quality.

### 1.7 Deviations and limitations of this review
- Absence of evidence is not proof of absence. Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar and Italian-language sources were not searched; OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar forward-citation checks are partial.
- Most abstracts were read, almost no full texts. Claims about design limits are inferences unless the table says otherwise.
- Researcher notes can conflict; resolutions are stated where they matter (sections 2, 4, 5.5, 9).
- "Not found" in this review means "not found in a limited search", not "does not exist".
- Several statements in the proposal and earlier reports rest on fetch-tool summaries or on the wiki note only; they are flagged, not corrected from memory.
- No protocol was registered.

## 4. Synthesis along the four Introduction storylines

**Evidence-strength words (reviewer's judgement, not tags).** *Strong* = several concordant verbatim abstracts from independent groups, or an abstract plus a wiki note that agree, and the design fits the claim. *Moderate* = a verbatim abstract ([ABSTRACT-READ]) or a wiki note ([W]) states the claim and the design fits. *Only-abstract* = the claim rests on a paraphrase-level abstract ([ABSTRACT-VIA-SEARCH], [ABSTRACT-PARAPHRASE]), on a truncated abstract, or on a secondhand fetch summary; wording may change after reading. *Weak* = design or region mismatch, preprint, conference abstract, grey literature, snippet or metadata only; do not use as the sole support. No new [PDF-CHECKED] was done in this round, so "Strong" never means "full text verified here". Independence matters: the Innsbruck, Obojes and Frank studies share authors and are not counted as independent.

### 4.1 Storyline 1: position change and rates of the forest line

**What is established.** Where imagery or resurvey exists, Alpine forest lines and treelines have moved upward at roughly one metre per year, but the number depends on which boundary is measured and over which window. The Dischma valley (Central Swiss Alps) advanced about 10 m per decade with a maximum of 42 m per decade between 1972/73 and 2012, and most new trees were infilling (Frei et al. 2023) [W]. In the Schnals valley (South Tyrol) the treeline advanced by +32 m in Landsat and +45 m in orthophotos between 1985 and 2022 in 15 of 16 areas (Menegaldo et al. 2025) [W]. In the French Pyrenees the forest line moved 0.9 m per year during 1851-1993 and 3.5 m per year during 1993-2010, with the closed forest line at 5.6 m per year (Delpouve et al. 2025), which shows that period and boundary change the rate several-fold. Globally, treelines advanced at 52 % of 166 sites and receded at 1 % (Harsch et al. 2009); 88.8 % of 143 sites ascended with a mean rate of 0.354 m per year, half of what warming alone would predict (Lu et al. 2021); and forest cover increased at 66 % of 477 locations (Hansson et al. 2021). The 0.354 m per year figure is now supported by an abstract; the earlier reports held it at snippet level. Most new forest near the line is not advance: 90 % of new forest above 1650 m was ingrowth in the Swiss Alps (Gehrig-Fasel et al. 2007), and 79 % of new-tree cells in Dischma were infilling (wiki note; abstract says "mostly"). In the south-western French Alps, most Landsat greening near the forest line was attributed to forest ingrowth and upward shift (Bayle et al. 2025). In Tyrol, the uppermost *P. cembra* in the Defereggen valley stand only about 140 m above the limit of nineteenth-century old trees, against about 400 m expected from 2.5 K of warming (Körner & Hiltbrunner 2024), and the Kauner Valley treeline rose about 65 m from about 1860 to 2245 m in 1980 (Nicolussi et al. 2005) [W].

**What is contested.** The boundary type changes the answer, and three families of evidence do not yet agree in magnitude or direction. Körner & Hiltbrunner (2024) describe uppermost trees that lag the isotherm by more than 200 m yet have advanced since the nineteenth century. Schindlbacher et al. (2026) report that the upper forest boundary at Austrian afforestation sites "has remained stable over the past two decades" despite regional warming. Landsat and greening studies report general forest expansion close to the forest line (Baglioni et al. 2025) and +32 m over 37 years in one South Tyrolean valley (Menegaldo et al. 2025). These measure different things (uppermost individuals, a closed boundary at planted sites, forest-fraction or index trends), over different windows (since the nineteenth century, two decades, 1984-2023) and at different sites, so they are not strict contradictions; but they mean a single "Tyrolean forest-line rate" is not defensible. The definition itself is contested: the strict thermal treeline (Körner & Hoch 2023) excludes edges set by land use or disturbance, whereas Malanson (2023) argues the definition leaves the krummholz and seedling zone "without terminology". An Italian preprint reports the timberline rising faster than the tree species line (1.6 vs 1.1 m per year in the earlier report), but it is not peer reviewed (Carrieri et al. 2026). Rates from Harsch (since 1900, 166 sites), Lu (143 sites, annual rates) and the Alpine single-boundary studies are not like-for-like.

**What is missing.** No imagery- or satellite-based forest-line position change for Austrian Tyrol was found; the nearest are South Tyrol (Menegaldo), the Italian Alps (Baglioni, Carrieri) and Berchtesgaden (a conference abstract, Maroschek). The forest-line elevation for Tyrol (the proposal's 1900-2300 m) is unresolved: the anchors found are 2245 m for the Kauner treeline in 1980, about 2500 m for the uppermost trees, and 1600-2100 m for afforestation sites. Numeric height and cover thresholds for forest line, timberline and tree species line have no peer-reviewed Austrian source in the notes. The Land Tirol statement that forest area has grown by about 800 ha per year since the 1960s, mostly in the alm region and subalpine zone, cannot be converted into a forest-line rate. The share of the gap between actual and potential forest line due to past land use versus demographic lag has not been measured.

### 4.2 Storyline 2: climate versus land use, browsing and disturbance

**What is established.** Most Alpine studies that rank drivers put land use ahead of climate for forest structure and treeline position. In Davos, land-use change contributed most to landscape forest structure change, "followed by changes in the disturbance regime, then changes in GDD" (Kulakowski et al. 2010). A pan-European expert comparison finds temperature alone insufficient and geomorphology, soil, microclimate and land-use history decisive (Cudlín et al. 2017) [W]. Dischma advance depended on aspect, species, microsite and land use in 1900 (Frei et al. 2023) [W]. A simulation of the Stubai Valley (Tyrol) places the present edge as mainly land-use constrained, with rapid expansion after abandonment regardless of warming (Marzini et al. 2025) [W]. The Innsbruck group's review calls the Central Austrian treeline "conservative" and reads seedling re-establishment as invasion of potential habitat after reduced grazing (Wieser et al. 2019) [W]. Abandonment is documented: 8 to 67 % of previously used land in the studied Eastern Central Alps regions was abandoned over 150 years (Tasser et al. 2007, search-summary level), greening aligns with the historical cadastral parcel structure in the Queyras (Bayle et al. 2025, PPG), and national alm statistics report that in 2000/03-2009 the alm fodder area declined by almost 10 % more than the number of alm farms, with the strongest decreases in Tirol, Steiermark, Vorarlberg and Kärnten (Groier 2011; the sentence is ambiguous and the survey is grey literature).

**What is contested.** The central dispute is real and unreconciled. Nicoud et al. (2025) [W] attribute an acceleration of larch establishment since the early 2000s in the southern French Alps to climate, with nineteenth-century abandonment only as a precondition (628 trees, one region); Marzini, Frei and Cudlín point the other way; and an Italian preprint finds land-use variables explained more than climate at nine sites (Carrieri et al. 2026, abstract via search summary). Feuillet et al. (2026) suggest that the answer depends on elevation zone: montane Pyrenean treelines show large shifts with a weak warming relation (rebound after pastoral abandonment), alpine treelines smaller shifts with a stronger warming relation. Delpouve et al. (2025) show timing matters (a four-fold rate increase after 1993). A precondition-plus-accelerator reading is shared by Nicoud and Bayle but has not been tested for Tyrol. The label "land-use-dominated" for Gehrig-Fasel et al. (2007) is stronger than the abstract, which names "both land use and climate change as likely drivers" and poses the former-pasture question as a question. Stability at Austrian afforestation sites (Schindlbacher 2026) is consistent with a land-use or demographic constraint but is not a test. Dullinger et al. (2004) find dispersal and invasibility limit simulated *P. mugo* expansion as much as 0-2 °C of warming [W], and no study separates land-use depression from demographic lag.

**Browsing and disturbance.** No treeline-specific quantification of browsing in the Alps was found. The Austrian game-influence monitoring (WEM) is grey literature and its sampling design (regeneration areas vs ecotone) was not found; the "41.8 % of Tyrol survey points in 2019-2021" in the proposal could not be reproduced, whereas a Land Tirol page gives 44.0 % for 2022-2024 in a sentence truncated by the fetch tool. Two context studies point in opposite directions (sapling numbers rising despite browsing in the Swiss National Park; unpalatable spruce favoured in Paneveggio; both URL-level, not DOI-checked). Avalanche and geomorphic disturbance enter through Kulakowski et al. (2010); Bebi et al. (2009), Leonelli et al. (2009, 2011) and Gorsic et al. (2025) are metadata or snippet level.

**What is missing.** A quantitative, uncertainty-aware apportioning of climate, land-use legacy, browsing and disturbance for Tyrolean forest-line change; a Tyrol-wide abandonment time series and spatial layer (only Bundesland-level alm statistics); an altitude-resolved ÖWI analysis; avalanche-track and ownership layers; and Eastern Alps coverage generally: a systematic review found "a lack of studies in the northeastern region of the Alps", although the abstract does not say whether Tyrol is included (Anselmetto et al. 2024).

### 4.3 Storyline 3: stress at the forest line and its detection

**What is established.** Growth at the treeline is sink-limited by low temperature rather than carbon-limited (Hoch & Körner 2012; Körner & Paulsen 2004), and the Innsbruck group concludes that treeline trees are "not carbon limited" (Wieser et al. 2019). Ring evidence in dry inner-Alpine valleys shows a species contrast: larch responds to temperature and has grown faster in recent decades, while *P. cembra* at high elevation shows a temperature response weaker than expected and reduced radial-growth days under high vapour-pressure deficit (Obojes et al. 2022, 2024) [W]; across five treeline ecotones, *P. cembra* growth and wood anatomy depended on moisture, especially at drier sites, and larch on temperature (Marotta et al. 2026; two Karwendel sites in Tyrol, two in South Tyrol, one in the Engadin). At Patscherkofel, growth followed July temperature until the mid-1980s and then departed from it, with "extreme climate events and/or increasing drought stress" suggested but untested (Oberhuber et al. 2008, search-summary level), and ring width correlated with a mild previous autumn and winter precipitation, consistent with winter desiccation (Oberhuber 2004). A frost-drought signature (January precipitation) appears in treeline alder (Oberhuber et al. 2022). Winter embolism has been shown at the Tyrolean timberline in spruce but not in stone pine (Mayr et al. 2003). Removing snow at 1700 m near Praxmar raised spring hydraulic damage and summer mortality in saplings of five species, not including *P. cembra* (Charra-Vaskou et al. 2026) [W]. The larch budmoth is the one biotic agent with a ring signature: synchronized Alpine outbreaks ceased after 1982 but local peaks returned in 2017-2018 (Büntgen et al. 2020), and the 2018 outbreak in one Swiss larch forest was detectable by satellite (Peters et al. 2020). On snow, station depth fell 8.4 % per decade across the Alps (Matiu et al. 2021) [W] and the Austrian model shows a 42-day loss of snow-cover duration that is significant only below about 2000 m (Olefs et al. 2020, prior note) [W]. Satellite detection works for severe closed-canopy stress (Sturm et al. 2022; Löw & Koukal 2020 [W]) but high-elevation conifers show weak, null or positive Sentinel-2 anomalies in 2018 and 2022 (Sturm 2022 reports damage at lower elevations; Dalponte et al. 2025 report "unusually green canopies" at higher elevations). Public Tyrolean tree-ring series in the ITRDB end 1967-1975; one Zenodo stand-mean file reaches 2021.

**What is contested.** (1) Whether drought has become co-limiting at the Tyrolean forest line. Support: the post-1980s divergence at Patscherkofel (Oberhuber 2008), legacies of recurrent drought in mature spruce and larch of a subalpine forest (Oberleitner et al. 2022; site elevation not confirmed), emerging drought sensitivity in the largest spruce at High Tatras treeline sites (Märker et al. 2025) [W], and larch growth decoupling from warming after 2000 in dense stands at 1723 m (Frank et al. 2026) [W]. Against: three years of rain exclusion did not reduce sap flow in treeline saplings (Wieser et al. 2019), water-use efficiency stayed constant and soil water "was not a limiting factor" in 1975-2010 (Wieser et al. 2016), and warming enhanced growth of solitary trees in an open stand (Gruber et al. 2022). The studies differ in age class, soil layer, outcome and period and should not be cited alone. (2) Whether high elevation is buffered: Sentinel-2 studies suggest so, but Noyer et al. (2026) report "a vanishing altitudinal gradient" in canopy resilience in Swiss protective forests (altitude belts not stated in the abstract). (3) Whether less snow is harmful or protective: snow-free saplings were damaged (Charra-Vaskou 2026), but snowmelt date was the most important factor for mortality in the Stillberg planting and snow-fungus mortality of *P. cembra* plantations is documented by title (Barbeito et al. 2012, earlier report: abstract read; Barbeito et al. 2013, metadata only), so the sign depends on microsite, species and life stage. (4) Independence: Obojes 2018/2022/2024 and Frank 2026 share authors (Tasser, Obojes, Oberhuber); note B counts "three independent groups" for the larch-temperature / stone-pine-moisture contrast, but only the Obojes cluster and Marotta et al. are distinct. Their sites are in South Tyrol (Vinschgau, Mazia) except Karwendel and Frank's North Tyrol stands. (5) *P. cembra* and budmoth: Cerrato et al. (2023) call stone pine immune to the defoliator, whereas a Graubünden notice (grey) says larvae move to stone pine when larch needles run out; not reconciled.

**What is missing.** No ring study of adult *P. cembra*, larch or spruce at the Tyrolean forest line analyses 2003, 2015, 2018 or 2022 as event years (Pichler & Oberhuber 2007 covers 2003 at a low-elevation site; Marotta's pointer years are not listed in the fetch summary). No frost-ring counts for 1990-2022 in Tyrol, no late-frost damage record for larch or spruce at the Alpine forest line (the only *P. cembra* case is one false-spring event in the High Tatras, Lukasová et al. 2025), no Tyrolean blue-intensity or MXD series (Cerrato 2023, 2025 are on the Italian side), no verified Austrian budmoth record after 1982, no Austria-specific snow trend at 1900-2300 m, and no Sentinel-2 or Landsat versus ring comparison at an Alpine forest line (the nearest are Oddi et al. 2022, where larch canopy gas exchange did not change in extreme years but stem growth fell 17 % in 2017, and Peters et al. 2020 for budmoth). A snow trend at the forest-line elevation sits where the evidence flips: strong spring and snow-cover decline below about 2000 m, no significant decline in the Austrian model above it, and few stations.

### 4.4 Storyline 4: detection and attribution methods

**What is established.** Landsat can show forest-line change of tens of metres: Random Forest on Landsat 5 / 8 underestimated the orthophoto-derived advance by about 13 m in Schnals (Menegaldo et al. 2025) [W], and trend products along the forest line exist for Italy (Baglioni et al. 2025). No published source states a validated minimum detectable shift: the one-pixel estimate (30 m plus geolocation error) is the project's own inference; Corimanya et al. (2025) infer a 20.2 m mean upslope shift from NDVI isolines (fetch summary) without validating sub-pixel claims, and very high resolution imagery gave 2-12 m shifts in the Tianshan (Luo & Dai 2013). Observation density can inflate NDVImax greening trends: "almost 50 %" of alpine greening above 2400 m was explained by observation density, with the bias "less pronounced" in the lower forest ecotone (Bayle et al. 2024) [W]; the wiki note puts the relative importance at 10-20 % at 1500-2400 m and 8 % in the forest ecotone, and the Swiss national NDVI analysis did not correct for it (Obuchowicz et al. 2024) [W]. For 3D change, repeat ALS at a Norwegian tree line estimated change only for domains of at least 1.5 ha (mean growth 0.19 m over six years; Næsset et al. 2019), photogrammetric surfaces smooth small and solitary trees (Næsset et al. 2021), sapling clusters were detectable with ALS from about 1.1 to 1.4 m at 22 pulses per m2 (Thapa et al. 2026), and a stereo test found small objects recovered at under 20 % of height for Pleiades (secondhand, Loghin et al. 2020). Land Tirol holds two statewide ALS epochs (2006-2010 and 2017-2023, at least 8 ground points per m2 in the second) and a 20 cm orthophoto archive with photos since the 1940s (official pages, fetch summary). Deep learning on aerial imagery delivers multi-epoch records: Swiss treelines 1946-2020 with a temporal contour-alignment loss (Nguyen et al. 2024) [W], dense forest well and sparse tree groups poorly on black-and-white images (Wang et al. 2022), and drone-based detection that rises with tree height (70 % above 50 cm, 86 % above 2 m; Carrieri et al. 2025) [W]. Validation methods are mature: non-spatial cross-validation underestimates error (Roberts et al. 2017; Ploton et al. 2020), and the area of applicability and kNNDM supply tools (Meyer & Pebesma 2021; Linnenbrink et al. 2024). Shared change-detection and map-error literature (Pontius & Lippitt 2006, Olofsson et al. 2014, LandTrendr) is covered in the evergreen review and not repeated here.

**What is contested.** (1) The size of the observation-density artefact: the 50 % figure applies to non-forest pixels above 2400 m, the forest ecotone is less affected, and Lewińska et al. (2025) found "no significant effect of data density" on trends of cumulative endmember fractions across 13 grassland sites (search summary), so the artefact may be metric-specific; the proposal should not use the 50 % figure to motivate corrections at 1900-2200 m. (2) Spatial cross-validation as a map-accuracy estimator is disputed (Wadoux et al. 2021, title only), so the validation target (map accuracy or process inference) must be stated. (3) The "no VHR satellite" claim is now false in 2D: WorldView-2 with RF and U-Net gave an upslope shift of 32.00 ± 4.00 m in Taiwan (Wang et al. 2026); only satellite-stereo height change is untested. (4) Geolocation: the Landsat 5 Collection 2 Tier 1 criterion of "<=12 m RMSE" (snippet) and the roughly 17 m CE90 for Landsat 8 / 9 in the earlier gaps report are different metrics and neither addresses TM in steep terrain.

**What is missing.** A published minimum detectable shift; an observation-density bootstrap at a forest line; a Landsat TM geolocation figure for mountain terrain; an Alpine topographic-correction benchmark for TM / ETM+ forest cover; a repeat-ALS forest-line study in the Eastern Alps; any satellite-stereo forest-line height change; historical aerial photo SfM for treeline height; a deep-learning forest-line study with Tyrolean imagery; a variance-partitioning or spatial-CV attribution study of Alpine forest-line change; and an observational counterfactual precedent (only simulation, iLand). "Not found" refers to a limited search; none of the topics has had a Scopus or Web of Science search.
